ABSTRACT
Small open language models can call tools, but they do not reason about what a call does: they delete
without asking, repeat writes, re-read state they already hold, and issue one call per turn where a
short program would do. We introduce EffectWorld, an executable world of effect-typed tools in
which every tool declares its effect (pure, read, write or destructive), whether it is idempotent, and
which resources it reads and writes. These declarations let a replay verifier decide whether a trace is
effect-safe without labels or judge models, let an executor run tool programs of several dependent
calls in one turn, and let a compiler-style optimizer turn wasteful traces into verified training signal;
together they make EffectWorld a benchmark of effect safety and call efficiency. Low-rank adapters
trained on EffectWorld data deliver large gains: on Qwen3-4B, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) raises
task success from 53% to 99% in distribution, from 52% to 81% on a held-out split with unseen
task kinds and distractor tools, and from 74% to 83% on an unseen online-shopping domain, with
about a third of the output tokens. The gains carry over to three model families, lifting Qwen3-1.7B,
Phi-4-mini and Falcon3-3B from 4–25% to 69–99% in distribution and by 26 to 55 points on the
held-out splits. On the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) the adapter matches the
base model’s live accuracy and raises multi-turn accuracy from 21.9 to 26.6, and a recipe with
near-miss declines lifts the BFCL average above the base model’s (70.0 against 68.7) while matching
its irrelevance detection. Along the way we uncover and fix a pitfall that in-domain evaluation alone
would miss: refusal templates in widely used public tool-use corpora teach a small model not to
call tools, cutting BFCL non-live accuracy from 88.0 to 31.4, and filtering them restores 84.8. We
also find that positive examples, not negative ones, carry the learning: suppression and preference
objectives on the verified negatives add nothing over an SFT-only control, while targeted positives
move exactly the behaviour they show. Efficiency transfers to unseen tools out of the box; effect
discipline does so only partly, and EffectWorld makes that remaining gap measurable for future work.
1
Introduction
A tool-calling agent is efficient when it reaches the intended final state with few calls, few turns and few generated
tokens, and safe when it never takes an irreversible action the user has not approved. Public benchmarks such as the
Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) mostly score whether a call is well formed (Patil et al., 2024, 2025).
They say little about whether an agent re-reads data it already has, serialises independent look-ups, or cancels a meeting
without asking. Tool protocols already annotate tools as read-only, destructive or idempotent (Anthropic, 2024; Model
Context Protocol contributors, 2025), yet small open models are not trained to act on such annotations, and, as we
found in our own first adapters, the annotations often never reach the model at all. The idea is older than agents:
type-and-effect systems let a compiler reason about what a piece of code may read or write (Lucassen and Gifford,
1988); we borrow it for tool calls.

We make tool effects first-class during data generation, training and evaluation. We build EffectWorld, an executable tool
world with a replay verifier, in which each tool carries an effect type, an idempotence flag and a footprint: the resources
it reads and writes, templated by its arguments. Effects let us state policies precisely, for example “confirm before any
destructive call” and “never repeat a non-idempotent write”. Footprints let a trace optimizer decide which calls can be
removed, merged or reordered without changing the outcome. The optimizer in turn converts every inefficient trace into
labelled negative examples.
Negative examples for tool agents are usually mined from model failures or produced by ad-hoc perturbation. Ours
differ in one respect: an efficiency negative is a call the optimizer has shown to be unnecessary, because replaying
the trace without it reaches the same final state with no new errors. We combine these with rule-based correctness
and policy negatives and train adapters to suppress the bad action at the exact prefix where it occurs, keeping the
span-localised recipe of LUNE (Liu et al., 2025), which trains only low-rank adapters (LoRA) on negative examples,
but replacing its objective with suppression by unlikelihood training (UL; Welleck et al., 2020) or negative preference
optimisation (NPO; Zhang et al., 2024a), since for an agent the negative target is an action that must not be produced.
We make five contributions.
1. EffectWorld, an effect-typed executable tool world with a replay verifier, a scripted user, a training domain
and two test-only splits: a hard split with unseen task kinds and distractor tools, and an online-shopping
(commerce) domain whose tools never appear in training (§§3.1–3.4).
2. Tool programs: a minimal $ref convention that lets one assistant turn express a dependency graph of calls
inside native tool-call blocks, so existing parsers and benchmarks keep working (§3.2).
3. A compiler-style trace optimizer with footprint-based legality, and a taxonomy of 19 negative classes derived
from its diffs, from rule perturbations and from policy checks, each mapped to the BFCL categories it should
affect (§3.5).
4. A controlled comparison of two suppression objectives (UL, NPO) against direct preference optimisation
(DPO) and a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) control, all continued from the same SFT adapter. With correctly
localised and scaled objectives none of them beats the control on any held-out split, and we document the three
loss-scaling defects that made a first round fail silently (§4.1, §5.6).
5. Two findings about data. Refusal templates in public tool-use data collapse a small model’s willingness to
call tools on BFCL while in-domain metrics look perfect, and filtering them fixes it (§6.1). Positives transfer
along their cue rather than their label: declining when a tool is absent does not teach declining an off-topic
request, declines that show the right cue restore BFCL irrelevance detection at a cost in confirmation, and
effect annotations in the schema move confirmation on unseen tools only a little. The pattern holds across
three model families (§§5.7–5.8).

Related work
Teaching language models to call tools began as a data problem. Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023) showed that a model
can annotate its own training text with calls to application programming interfaces (APIs) and keep those that lower the
loss on the following tokens; ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) interleaves free-text reasoning with actions so that a model can
plan a call, read its result and decide what to do next. The corpora that followed scale this up with synthesis rather
than self-annotation: ToolLLM (Qin et al., 2024) collects thousands of real web APIs and generates multi-step solution
paths with a depth-first search over calls, Gorilla (Patil et al., 2024) fine-tunes on API documentation with retrieval,
ToolACE (Liu et al., 2024b) and APIGen (Liu et al., 2024c) synthesise API definitions and dialogues and keep only the
examples that pass format, execution and semantic checks, and NVIDIA’s Nemotron-Agentic (NVIDIA, 2025) and
the Hermes function-calling corpus (Nous Research, 2024) provide hundreds of thousands of single- and multi-turn
trajectories that include tool responses. Small models trained on such data are competitive function callers: TinyAgent
(Erdogan et al., 2024) fine-tunes 1.1B and 7B models to emit LLMCompiler-style call plans on a laptop, and Hammer
(Lin et al., 2025) adds irrelevance examples and masks function names after finding that models overfit to the naming
conventions of their training data. We use three of these corpora as the public part of our mix, and §6.1 shows that their
refusal-style turns are not harmless: filtered one way they teach a small model not to call, filtered the other way they
teach it not to decline. What none of these corpora carries is a statement of what a call does to the world, so a trajectory
can be checked for well-formedness but not for whether a write was necessary, repeated or confirmed.
Evaluation has moved in the same direction, from the syntax of a call to the state it leaves behind. BFCL (Patil
et al., 2025) compares abstract syntax trees of predicted and reference calls and adds categories for relevance (call
when a suitable function exists) and irrelevance (decline when none does) and for multi-turn interaction; it is the

drive the optimizer that produces training data.
A second line of work makes calls cheaper, either by not making them or by issuing several at once. SMART (Qian
et al., 2025a) trains agents not to call tools for questions they can answer from their own knowledge. LLMCompiler
(Kim et al., 2024) has the model emit a dependency graph of calls that an executor runs in parallel, and CodeAct (Wang
et al., 2024) lets the model act by writing executable Python, which subsumes graphs, loops and arithmetic. Our tool
programs are deliberately weaker than either of these two: a later call may only select a field of an earlier result through
a $ref, and nothing else is computed in the program. The restriction keeps programs inside the native tool-call format,
so the same adapter answers stepwise benchmarks, and it keeps every program small enough for a static analysis to
decide which calls are dead, redundant or reorderable. That analysis is where the compiler analogy becomes literal.
Type-and-effect systems (Lucassen and Gifford, 1988) annotate expressions with the store regions they read and write
so that a compiler can tell pure computations from effectful ones and decide which may be moved; our declarations are
the same device for API calls, and the optimizer’s legality condition is the read–write conflict rule of such systems,
checked by replay rather than proved.
The concern that an agent may change the world in ways nobody asked for has been studied under several names. In
reinforcement learning it appears as side-effect avoidance, where an agent is penalised for making states unreachable
(Krakovna et al., 2019), and as reversibility, which can be learned from the temporal order of observations and used
to steer away from irreversible actions (Grinsztajn et al., 2021). For language-model agents the question has mostly
been asked at evaluation time: ToolEmu (Ruan et al., 2024) replaces real tools with a language model that emulates

the boundary (Trofimov and Novikov, 2026), and we are not aware of a small open model trained to act on them. Our
results bear on that gap from the training side. The adapters learn effect safety for the tools they were trained on, but as
§5 shows, an adapter that never sees the annotations attaches the confirmation habit to tool names and loses much of it
on unseen tools, which is why one of our recipes (§4.2) renders the annotations into the tool descriptions.
The training objectives we compare come from the literature on learning from negative examples. Unlikelihood training
(Welleck et al., 2020) lowers the probability of tokens that should not appear, negative preference optimisation (Zhang
et al., 2024a) bounds the gradient of such a term with a reference model so that suppression cannot run away, and
DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and odds-ratio preference optimisation (ORPO; Hong et al., 2024) contrast a chosen and a
rejected continuation at a shared prefix. LUNE (Liu et al., 2025) applies the recipe to adapters: a frozen backbone, a
low-rank adapter and a loss restricted to the span of the negative example. We keep that recipe and change the objective,
because for an agent the negative is an action that must not be produced rather than text to be replaced; tool unlearning
(Cheng and Amiri, 2025) is the extreme case, removing a tool from the model’s repertoire altogether. For agents,
negatives have mostly been whole trajectories: NAT (Wang et al., 2025b) fine-tunes on failed trajectories marked as
such, ETO (Song et al., 2024) pairs an agent’s own failures with successes for DPO, and Agent-FLAN (Chen et al.,
2024) adds negative samples against hallucinated actions. Our pairs are localised to one turn and labelled by the class of
mistake. The closest alternatives for reducing unnecessary calls are reinforcement learning with format, correctness and
call-count rewards (Qian et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a), which need on-policy rollouts and an executor in the loop;
our negatives are an offline substitute labelled by the verifier, and §5.6 reports that, in this setting, they do not improve
on supervised fine-tuning with the same positives. One failure both approaches share is relevant here: an agent that
should ask rather than guess. Wang et al. (2025c) trace the guessing to next-token training, which teaches a model to fill
a missing argument rather than ask, and Qian et al. (2024) train agents on explicit clarification dialogues and show that
vagueness has to be taught from positive examples; our held-out ambiguous-contact tasks, on which no variant learned
to ask, reach the same conclusion from the negative side.
3
Data
This section describes the data. EffectWorld, the executable world in which tasks are generated, run and scored, consists
of effect-typed tools (§3.1), an executor for tool programs (§3.2), task generators with a replay verifier and a scripted
user (§3.3), and three evaluation splits (§3.4). The training sets combine EffectWorld traces, the negative examples a
trace optimizer derives from them (§3.5), and public tool-use data (§3.6).
3.1
EffectWorld: effect-typed tools and effect safety
The world is built from tools whose behaviour is declared rather than described. A tool is a JSON-schema function plus
metadata that the model does not see by default: an effect, an idempotence flag, read and write footprints, a simulated
latency, and optionally the name of the single-item tool it batches.
The training domain, workspace, is an office assistant: 18 tools over contacts, a calendar, a mailbox and a task list,
with which the user asks it to find, schedule, move and cancel events, read, send and reply to email, and create or
complete tasks. Two of the tools are batch variants of single-item tools (get_contacts, complete_tasks). Its state
is generated from a seed.
The effect e ∈{PURE, READ, WRITE, DESTRUCTIVE} says what a call does to the world (Table 1). A PURE tool
computes its result from its arguments alone, a READ tool looks at the state without changing it, a WRITE tool changes
the state in a way that can be corrected later, and a DESTRUCTIVE tool makes a change that cannot be taken back: it

These declarations do three jobs that a schema of names and parameters cannot. First, they turn safety into a checkable
property of a trace, which we call effect safety: a trace is effect-safe if every destructive call follows a proposal the
user confirmed, no non-idempotent write is repeated, and every write is preceded by a read of the state it changes
(Figure 2). The verifier decides this by replaying the recorded calls against the declarations, with no judge model and no
human labels; the base model’s 94 unconfirmed deletions and sends on the in-distribution test split (§5) are effect-safety
violations counted this way. The behaviour of a model whose traces are effect-safe we call its effect discipline. Second,
they turn efficiency into a decidable property: footprints make it possible to prove that a read is redundant, that a call
is dead, or that two calls may run in the same turn, so the optimizer and every negative it produces follow from the
declarations rather than from heuristics about tool names. Third, they are a generalisation handle: a policy stated
over effects is the same policy for a calendar and for a shop, so it can transfer to unseen tools, but only if the model is
shown the effect. Our first adapters were not (the schema the model sees is name, description and parameters), and
the commerce results in §5 show the consequence; the Model Context Protocol’s tool annotations (Model Context
Protocol contributors, 2025) are the practical place to carry this information, and §5.7 tests rendering it into the schema.
Declaring effects is cheap, one enum, one flag and a footprint pattern per tool, compared with writing judges or labelling
traces.
The model, by contrast, is told the rules in words. The system prompt states five policies: confirm before any irreversible
action, never repeat an action that creates, sends or pays, read current data before modifying it, ask when required
information is missing, and do not call tools when none is needed.
3.2
Tool programs
An assistant turn may contain k ≥1 native <tool_call> blocks. Call i may use the result of an earlier call
j < i of the same turn inside any argument value through a reference, a $ref, written "$j" or as a path such as
"$j.events[0].id"; [*] projects a field over a list. We call a turn with several dependent calls a tool program, and
running a task this way program mode. In stepwise mode the model makes one call per turn and reads each result before
the next call. No new top-level keys are introduced, so parsers for the common <tool_call> JSON format used by
Hermes and Qwen models, and BFCL’s parallel categories, are unaffected. Program mode is announced by one sentence
in the system prompt; without it the executor rejects references with a structured error and the model must call stepwise.

The executor builds a graph from the references and from footprint conflicts (a write followed by a read or write of an
overlapping resource is ordered), runs it in topological levels, substitutes resolved values, and returns one observation
per call. Simulated latency is the sum over levels of the slowest call in the level. A destructive call is legal only in a turn
that follows the user’s confirmation, which acts as a barrier.
3.3
Tasks, verifier and scripted user
Tasks are generated, not written, so that every one of them comes with a reference solution. A task is a user utterance, a
seed, goal predicates over the final state (event created, email sent with given body words, tasks completed, ...), facts
the final answer must contain, and flags for confirmation and missing information. Tasks come in nine kinds, each a
family of requests produced by one generator, such as cancelling an event or replying to an email (Appendix A lists
them). A generator returns the task, an optimal plan and a naive plan; executing a plan in the world records its trace,
and the trace of the optimal plan is the task’s gold trace. Naive plans are correct but wasteful: per-item calls instead of
batch tools, look-ups of values the user already gave, detail reads whose results are never used, re-reads after writes,
and one call per turn. On the test split naive plans make 4.52 calls per task against 2.11 for optimal plans. Figure 3
shows one task in its naive, stepwise and program-mode forms.
Because the effects of every call are declared, success can be decided by replay rather than by a judge. The verifier
replays a trajectory’s calls on a fresh state, so it scores scripted and model trajectories alike. A task succeeds when
all goal predicates hold, the final answer contains the required facts (negative facts such as “nothing scheduled” are
matched by a negation lexicon), no entity outside the goal changed (such a change is collateral and fails the task), and
the trace is effect-safe. Effect-safety violations are also recorded individually (unconfirmed destructive calls, duplicate
writes and stale-state writes). It also records excess calls and turns against the optimal plan, tool errors, and simulated
latency.
The same replay scores model rollouts, which talk to a scripted user that confirms proposals, supplies missing
information once, and otherwise ends the conversation; its replies are deterministic, so two models that ask the same
question receive the same answer.
3.4
Evaluation splits
Training uses only the workspace generator; evaluation deliberately does not. The splits below, with the generators, the
verifier and the scripted user, are released with the code and can serve as a benchmark of effect safety and call efficiency
on their own: tasks are executable, every task has a verified reference solution, and scoring needs no judge model. We
evaluate on three splits that share no seeds with training:
• The test split (498 tasks): the nine training kinds.
• The hard split (775 tasks): the workspace state with six distractor tools from foreign domains (two of them
effectful), the nine training kinds, and eight unseen kinds that target the negative classes: an ambiguous first

The commerce domain is an online-shopping assistant. Its state holds a product catalogue with prices and stock, the
user’s cart, past orders with their delivery status, and saved addresses and payment methods. Its 17 tools search and
read products, edit the cart, list and read orders, place an order (which pays for it), change an order’s delivery address,
cancel an order and request a refund. None of them shares a name, an argument or an entity type with the workspace
tools, and no commerce trajectory is used in training. The 13 task kinds range from look-ups (the price of a product, a
comparison of two prices, the status of an order) and cart edits, including adding several items with one batch call, to the
three irreversible actions, buying, cancelling and refunding, each of which must follow a confirmed proposal. In four
kinds the correct outcome is that nothing irreversible happens: an order that has already shipped cannot be cancelled, a
product that is missing from the catalogue or out of stock cannot be bought, and one request needs no tool at all.
We chose a shop for three reasons. Its effects have the same structure as the workspace’s, with reads, reversible writes
and irreversible actions, so the five policies of the system prompt apply unchanged: a model that has learnt them
over effects should follow them here, whereas a model that has learnt them over tool names has nothing to go on. Its
irreversible actions move money, so an unconfirmed call is a failure that needs no argument. And retail is one of the
domains of widely used agent benchmarks such as τ-bench (Yao et al., 2024), so the behaviour it tests is the one agents
are deployed for. The commerce split therefore measures transfer: whether what the adapters learn about efficiency and
effect safety on workspace tools carries over to tools they have never seen.
3.5
Trace optimizer and negative classes
The negative variants of §4.2 train on pairs. A pair is a conversation prefix followed by two possible next turns, a good
one and a bad one, and it is labelled with the class of mistake the bad turn makes. Pairs come from three sources: the
trace optimizer described next, which finds the calls a wasteful trace did not need; rules that turn a correct turn into a
specific mistake; and public tool-use data, for one class. Table 9 in Appendix A lists the classes, and Figures 3, 4 and 5
show examples.
The optimizer starts from a naive trace, which reaches the goal but makes unnecessary calls (§3.3). It replays the trace
to recover each call’s arguments, result and footprints, then applies five passes, repeating them until none changes the
trace any more. Their names follow the compiler optimisations they imitate:
• Constant folding removes a pure call whose result already follows from its arguments, such as resolving a
date the user gave as 2026-04-02 (class lookup_of_known_value).
• Redundant-read elimination removes a read that repeats an earlier one with no write to the same resources
in between, or that returns nothing not already known (redundant_read).
• Dead-call elimination removes a read whose result is never used by a later call or by the answer, such as
opening an email whose content is not needed (dead_call).
• Batching replaces several calls of a single-item tool with one call of its batch variant, such as completing five
tasks one by one instead of in one call (unbatched).
• Scheduling, in program mode only, packs calls into as few turns as their data dependencies, footprint conflicts
and confirmation barriers (§3.2) allow (serialized_calls).
To know which earlier result an argument came from, the optimizer matches argument values against earlier results;
when several results contain the value, it prefers the one that also contains something the user mentioned.
A pass may only remove or move what the declarations prove harmless. Write and destructive calls are never removed
(batching only merges them into their batch variant) and never moved across a confirmation barrier. A rewrite is
accepted only if replaying the optimized trace reaches the same final state with no new tool errors. On every split, the
optimizer reduces each naive trace to exactly the number of calls of the optimal plan, in both modes.
Every change the optimizer makes yields a pair. The naive and the optimized trace agree up to some turn and differ
there: the conversation up to that turn is the shared prefix, the naive turn is the bad action, the optimized turn the good
one, and the pass that made the change gives the class.

The other pairs keep a gold turn as the good action and replace it with a deliberately wrong turn at the same prefix.
Thirteen rules each produce one kind of mistake. Ten concern correctness: calling the wrong tool or one that does
not exist, dropping a required argument, changing an argument’s value or its type, calling a tool when none is needed,
answering a question without looking anything up (missing_call), claiming that a change was made without making it
(premature_answer), acting on a guessed value instead of asking for missing information (guessed_missing_info),
and using $refs when program mode was not announced (ref_without_capability). Three concern effects and
break the three rules of effect safety: performing the destructive action at the point where the gold turn asks for
confirmation (unconfirmed_destructive), repeating a non-idempotent write (duplicate_write), and changing
or deleting an entity by a guessed identifier without reading it first (stale_state_write). The last class, refusal,
applies a rule to public data instead of world traces: at a public prefix whose gold action is a call, the bad action is a
templated refusal such as “I don’t have access to a tool that can do that” (§6.1). In total there are 19 classes. Eighteen
are built on EffectWorld traces, five by the optimizer and thirteen by rules; we call them world classes. The nineteenth,
refusal, is built on public data.
3.6
Public data and training mixes
The SFT data mix combines gold EffectWorld trajectories with public tool-use trajectories, and it exists in two versions.
The raw mix combines 5,976 gold world trajectories (2,988 training tasks, each in program and stepwise mode) with
15,001 public trajectories sampled from the tool-calling and interactive subsets of Nemotron-Agentic-v1 (NVIDIA,
2025), Hermes function calling (Nous Research, 2024) and ToolACE (Liu et al., 2024b), 20,977 examples in total. The
clean mix (§6.1) drops 1,751 public trajectories that contain refusal templates, caps first-turn no-call examples at 5%
of each public source and removes an arithmetic item from the world’s no-tool kind (irrelevant in Appendix A),
leaving 20,975 examples. The pairs for the negative variants cap each of 17 world classes at 400 (6,580 pairs); the clean
mix adds 1,000 refusal pairs, for 7,580 (996 of the refusal pairs survive tokenisation).

This section describes how the models are trained (§4.1), the recipes that produce them (§4.2), the further backbones
(§4.3) and how they are evaluated (§4.4).
4.1
Training objectives
Unless stated otherwise (§4.3), all models share a frozen Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 backbone (Qwen Team, 2025) and
train LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) with rank 16, α = 16 and dropout 0.05 on the attention projections and the up
and down projections of the multilayer perceptron (MLP) (26.0M trainable parameters), following LUNE’s defaults:
AdamW, 5% warm-up and cosine decay, weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at 1.0, bfloat16 precision, sequences up
to 4,096 tokens.

same adapter serves both.

Every model in this paper is produced by one of the recipes in Table 2. Names with a subscript are SFT recipes: they

• Base is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 without an adapter.
• SFTraw is SFT on the raw mix for two epochs (1,269 steps of 32 sequences). It is the first model we trained
and shows what the public data does on its own (§6.1).
• SFTclean is the same recipe on the clean mix. It is the reference recipe: every other recipe below changes
exactly one thing relative to it.
• SFTdecline replaces 10% of the world training tasks (299 of 2,987) with tasks in which the tool the request
needs has been removed from the tool list, so the only correct answer is a short decline that names the missing
capability.
• SFTeffects keeps the clean mix but writes each tool’s declared effect and idempotence into its description, in
the style of the Model Context Protocol annotations, and renames the eighteen workspace tools in one of two
synonym dialects for a third of the world trajectories (1,997 of 5,976), so that the confirmation habit cannot
attach to a name.

serve it. Candidates come from public data only: a public request whose first turn is answered by a call loses
every tool it came with and is offered one to three tools from other public trajectories that are close to it in
topic (cosine similarity of term frequency–inverse document frequency vectors of tool names and descriptions)
but are not near-duplicates of the tool that was called. The base model answers each candidate; we keep those
it declines and use its own answer as the target, so a candidate whose offered tool might serve the request after
all is dropped. 1,500 of these replace as many public trajectories of the clean mix.
• The second-stage variants continue SFTclean for 300 steps with a fresh optimizer. The Control continues SFT
only; Unlikelihood (UL), NPO and DPO add the objectives of §4.1 on the clean mix’s pairs, with half of their
micro-batches still plain SFT, so they differ from the control only in the negative term. Two ablations rerun
Unlikelihood with the pairs restricted by class.

4.4
Evaluation and compute
All evaluations share one serving stack. World evaluations serve merged weights with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023),
render prompts with the training template, decode greedily, allow 12 model turns, and run every task in program and
stepwise mode. NESTful (1,861 sequences) is scored structurally in program mode after mapping its variable references
onto $refs.
BFCL v4 is run on its three offline groups, 4,441 prompts in all, with the official handler for each model. Non-live
prompts are written by the benchmark’s authors and scored by comparing the abstract syntax tree of each call with a
reference. They come in four categories: simple (one function; in Python, Java or JavaScript), multiple (one of several
functions must be chosen), parallel (several calls of one function) and parallel-multiple; irrelevance prompts offer only
functions that do not fit, and the correct answer is to call none. Live prompts were contributed by users and cover the
same categories, plus relevance prompts in which a function does fit. Multi-turn conversations run against stateful
simulated back-ends, in a base setting and with a missing function, missing parameters or a long context. We report
BFCL’s own accuracies per group.
Differences in success rates and accuracies are given in percentage points. For every difference between two models
quoted in the text we computed a 95% paired bootstrap confidence interval (2,000 resamples), pairing the two models’
runs on the same task and mode or on the same BFCL prompt. To keep the text readable, these intervals are collected in
Tables 12 and 13 (Appendix D); every difference listed there is significant, meaning its interval excludes zero, unless
the text marks it as not significant. Differences quoted without an interval, such as stepwise gaps or per-kind changes,
are descriptive. The intervals cover task sampling, not seed variation; §7 reports a second training seed.
Every job runs on a single A100 40 GB. An SFT recipe takes about 10.2 GPU-hours in three time-limited, self-resuming
slices (about 30 s per step, 27.5 GB peak). Each second-stage variant takes 2.5 to 4 GPU-hours, a world evaluation
about 10 minutes, and BFCL about 45 minutes.

5.6
Variants trained with negatives
Every variant continues from SFTclean for 300 steps with the protocol of §4.1; the control continues SFT only, so the
variants differ from it only in the negative term. Table 3 summarises the comparison, and Tables 4, 5 and 11 give the
detail. Differences below are against the control unless stated otherwise.
In distribution, the objectives do no harm. Every variant stays at or above 99% on the test split, and the validation NLL
of the positives stays within 1.4× the control’s.
On the hard split they do not fix the failures they target. No variant asks which of two same-named contacts is meant
(0% on both ambiguous-contact kinds). Both suppression variants act on non-existent senders more often than the
control (2–6% success against 17%), and the kind with two same-titled events does not move. Overall hard-split success
falls by 3.5 points under unlikelihood and by 3.9 under NPO in program mode, and by 9.3 and 5.3 points stepwise.
On the unseen domain suppression costs 20.8 points under unlikelihood and 12.4 under NPO. The losses concentrate
where a suppressed class has a close look-alike. The unbatched class penalises adding items one at a time, and the
kind that adds several items to the cart falls from 96% to 30% under unlikelihood. Reporting that a requested product
does not exist falls from 72% to 28% and 46%.
What the negatives do change is how readily the model calls. Unlikelihood gains 1.6 points of BFCL live accuracy
and 3.8 points of multi-turn accuracy, most clearly on multi-turn base (5.0 points) and live multiple (2.2 points). Its
irrelevance detection falls from 77.5 to 66.3, and NPO’s to 58.3. Of the 240 irrelevance prompts, the control declines
186, unlikelihood 159 and NPO 140 (Table 7). The refusal class, which contrasts a call with a templated refusal, is
the largest class (13% of all pairs), and its direction is the one the model follows. The control’s own 300 SFT steps
change nothing measurable relative to SFTclean: 0.3 points lower on the hard split, 0.4 higher on commerce and 4.0
lower on BFCL multi-turn base, none of them significant.
DPO, the pairwise objective, is the most careful variant but not the most successful. It makes no effect-safety violation on
the hard split, 5 unconfirmed destructive calls on commerce against the control’s 24, and fewer excess calls everywhere.
Its success is nonetheless lower, by 2.6 points on the hard split and 16.6 on commerce. On BFCL it gains nothing on
live or multi-turn prompts and loses 8.3 points of irrelevance detection.

One earlier result belongs here because it explains how the objectives above were calibrated. The same variants were
first trained from SFTraw with three defects in the objectives of §4.1:
1. The suppression span ran to the end of the turn, so the closing tag and the end-of-turn token, which the good
turn needs as well, were suppressed too.
2. β = 0.1 was applied to per-token mean log-probabilities instead of sums, so the loss kept pushing the span
down until it was about 30 nats per token below the reference.
3. The reference was the backbone. The SFT adapter already gives the bad spans about 7 nats per token less
log-probability than the backbone does, so a correctly scaled loss would have been saturated at step 0.
Every negative variant lost to its control (hard split 36.6–78.6% against 82.6%; commerce 15.9–65.1% against 80.3%).

unclosed. We report this round because its failure, a model taught not to close its calls, is invisible in the training curves,
where the negative loss went to zero, and appears only in held-out rollouts.
5.7
Recipes that change the data
Two findings above point at data, not objectives: the adapters decline less than the base on BFCL’s irrelevance prompts
(75.0 against 88.8) because the refusal filter of the clean mix removed the examples that taught declining, and they
confirm less reliably on unseen tools (24 against 7 unconfirmed destructive calls on commerce) because the effect
declarations never reach the model. The SFTdecline and SFTeffects recipes (§4.2) each change one thing in SFTclean to
address one of them: SFTdecline adds tasks whose only correct answer is a decline because the needed tool is missing,
and SFTeffects shows the effect declarations in the tool descriptions while renaming the tools, so that the confirmation
habit cannot attach to a name.
Neither of these two levers reaches the base on its own target; a third, SFTnear-miss, motivated by the analysis of §6.2, is
discussed at the end of this subsection. SFTdecline leaves BFCL irrelevance detection exactly where SFTclean had it and
lowers it by 8.0 points on live prompts. A “tool absent, so decline” positive does not transfer to BFCL’s cue, where the
functions are present and the request does not match them (§6.2). It does generalise in a direction we had not aimed
at. On the hard split it is the best model so far, 2.2 points above SFTclean in program mode and 4.1 stepwise, with no
effect-safety violation, chiefly because it stops acting on non-existent senders (19% to 58% success). It is also the
closest to the base on non-live BFCL accuracy (87.0 against 88.0, and 1.2 points above SFTclean). On the commerce split,
however, it loses 4.8 points. It makes 0.37 fewer calls per task but confirms less often (24 to 42 unconfirmed destructive
calls), as if “decline” had generalised to “do less”. SFTeffects moves confirmation transfer in the intended direction but
not far: 24 to 19 unconfirmed destructive calls (fewer violations per task, but not significantly) and purchases made
after a confirmation from 32% to 50%. Nothing else changes except a loss of 5.0 points on BFCL multi-turn base and
the best NESTful name F1 (0.55). Rendering the annotations helps only when the training data also shows destructive
tools under many names; a third of the trajectories in two dialects is evidently too little, and the base model’s habit
of asking before any purchase remains the stronger prior. Hammer (Lin et al., 2025) reports the same attachment of
function-calling behaviour to naming conventions and counters it by masking function names during training.
SFTnear-miss supplies the cue that SFTdecline missed: an offered tool from the request’s own topic that cannot serve it.
Of the 6,000 candidates, the base model declined 3,831, and 1,500 of these replaced public trajectories. It is the first
recipe to match the base model on BFCL irrelevance detection: 89.6 against 88.8 for the base, a difference that is not
significant, and 75.0 for SFTclean, a gain of 14.6 points. It declines 215 of the 240 irrelevance prompts, the base 213
(Table 7). Live irrelevance detection rises by 3.2 points but stays 3.5 points below the base. Non-live and live accuracy
rise as well, to 86.7 and 77.9, so its BFCL average of 70.0 is the only one above the base’s 68.7 (Table 3). On the hard
split it gains 1.4 points and, like SFTdecline, stops acting on non-existent senders (19% to 51% success). The price is
again paid on the unseen domain. Commerce success falls by 8.8 points, back to the base’s level, because the model
now refunds and buys without asking: unconfirmed destructive calls rise from 24 to 64, refunds of delivered orders fall

Other backbones
Table 6 compares the base models and recipes of the three backbones of §4.3 with Qwen3-4B. The base models leave

split and 13% of the commerce split, with 0.44 tool errors per task. Phi-4-mini rarely emits a tool call in its native
format: in EffectWorld it makes 0.15 to 0.34 calls per task. On BFCL the official decoder finds a call in none of its
answers. 86% of them are prose, typically an explanation with the call written as a Python code block, and the rest are
untagged JSON or calls opened with a doubled <|tool_call|> tag that the decoder cannot parse. It therefore scores
0 on every category that needs a call and 100 on irrelevance, because BFCL counts any answer without a parseable
call as a correct decline. To separate format from ability we re-scored its answers with BFCL’s own checker after a
lenient decoding step that also reads calls written as code, untagged JSON and doubled tags. The base model then
expresses a call in 60 to 68% of the prompts that need one and reaches 24.7 non-live and 39.2 live accuracy. All
reported numbers use the official decoder, so that the fine-tuned model is judged in the same way, and our own parser in
EffectWorld accepts all three forms. Falcon3 calls readily but wastefully, with 1.75 excess calls per task on the test
split, and declines only 35% of BFCL’s irrelevance prompts. SFT carries over to every family in distribution and on the
hard split, less so to the unseen domain. SFTclean lifts in-distribution success to 99% for Qwen3-1.7B and Falcon3-3B
and to 69% for Phi-4-mini, whose remaining failures are wrong plans rather than format errors, such as an email sent
without a recipient. On the hard split it adds 54.7, 32.0 and 52.1 points for Qwen3-1.7B, Phi-4-mini and Falcon3-3B,
and on commerce 41.0, 25.8 and 27.2 points. Commerce success nonetheless stays between 35% and 54%, far below
Qwen3-4B’s 83%: the smaller the model, the less of the training domain carries over to unseen tools. The confirmation
habit fails to transfer in every family. On commerce the three base models make 1, 2 and 2 unconfirmed destructive
calls and their SFTclean adapters 45, 6 and 39, the same pattern as Qwen3-4B (7 against 24).
On BFCL the families diverge. Phi-4-mini gains most, from 0 to 74.5 non-live and 63.8 live accuracy, and the lenient
re-scoring shows where the gain comes from. About a third of the non-live gain is format alone, since the base model
reaches 24.7 once its answers are decoded leniently. The rest comes from two behaviours that fine-tuning teaches. The
model now answers 86 to 97% of call prompts with a call, against 60 to 68% even under lenient decoding, so it stops
answering in prose. And it gets the structure of the calls right: parallel and parallel-multiple accuracy rise from 12.0
and 6.5 under lenient decoding to 78.0 and 74.5, because the base model writes one call or a loop where several calls
are needed. Its irrelevance detection falls from 87.5 under lenient decoding to 73.8, the same shift towards calling seen
in the other families. Falcon3-3B gains 26.5 points of non-live and 12.7 of live accuracy and keeps its low irrelevance
detection (34.2 against 34.6, not significant). Qwen3-1.7B loses: its irrelevance detection collapses from 81.2 to 35.4
and its live irrelevance detection from 73.4 to 23.8, the effect of the refusal filter (§6.1) at a much larger scale, and its
live accuracy falls from 74.5 to 67.1.
SFTdecline repeats on every family what it showed on Qwen3-4B, more strongly the smaller the model: declining
generalises to doing less. It cuts effect-safety violations on commerce to 0.026, 0.009 and 0.000 per task and raises
irrelevance detection by 8.3 and 5.0 points on Qwen3-1.7B and Phi-4-mini, but it does so by calling less. On commerce
Phi-4-mini’s calls per task fall from 1.96 to 1.01 and Falcon3-3B’s from 6.20 to 0.01, so Falcon3-3B with this recipe
almost never calls an unseen tool and its commerce success falls from 35.4% to 17.4%. Phi-4-mini also loses 29.3
points in distribution, and Falcon3-3B 21.5 points on the hard split.

6.2
When fine-tuning beats the base model on BFCL, and when it does not
Only SFTnear-miss beats the base model on the BFCL average, and it was designed after the analysis below. For the
other recipes the average hides two opposite movements. Counted per prompt over the single-turn categories, SFTclean
answers 157 prompts correctly that the base model gets wrong and 272 wrongly that the base model gets right. Half
of the 272 (36 non-live and 98 live) are irrelevance prompts on which it calls a function instead of declining. The
remainder are spread thinly over the call categories.
The gains come where BFCL rewards something EffectWorld teaches. On multi-turn base SFTclean solves 35.5% of
conversations against the base model’s 26.0%, on the turns that withhold a required parameter 22.5% against 15.5%,
and on live simple calls 82.6% against 78.3%. These are the behaviours of the world’s tasks: carrying state across turns,
asking for information that is missing instead of inventing it, and calling directly when one function fits.

The irrelevance losses have one shape. In the failures we sampled, the model calls the function closest to the request in
topic and fills the arguments the request does not provide, the failure that Wang et al. (2025c) attribute to next-token
training. Asked for an external rate of return, it calls a tool that computes the internal one; asked for compound interest,
it calls a mortgage calculator; asked for a lion’s dominant genetic trait, it passes "Lion" as a variant identifier to a
variant-frequency look-up. The training data never shows this situation. Every one of the 14,999 public trajectories in
the clean mix contains a call. The world’s no-call tasks are general requests (the no-tool training kind) or workspace-
flavoured requests that ask for no action (the near-domain kind of the hard split, which SFTclean declines without
exception); in neither does an offered tool look applicable without being so. A request that matches a tool’s topic but
not its function is therefore always answered by a call in training, and the base model’s calibration on such prompts,
presumably learnt from data of this kind, is overwritten. SFTdecline confirms that the cue matters: its declines are
triggered by a tool that is absent, and on BFCL, where a function with a plausible name is always present, it declines no
more often on non-live irrelevance prompts and less often on live ones (74.4 to 66.4). SFTnear-miss supplies the missing
cue directly, and it works: irrelevance detection rises from 75.0 to 89.6, level with the base model, although the same
contrast costs confirmation on unseen tools (§5.7). The cue is the limited-functionality setting of ToolBeHonest (Zhang
et al., 2024b), and When2Call (Ross et al., 2025) reports that a preference objective over call, ask and decline options
teaches this decision better than SFT, a contrast with our negatives worth testing on this cue.
The remaining gap on calls is small, and little of it is about effects. The base model already scores 95.5 on simple
Python calls and 94.5 on multiple-function prompts, so there is little room above it. The rest of the losses are on
parallel-multiple prompts (90.0 to 84.0, mostly a wrong number of calls), on Java and JavaScript prompts whose
argument types our JSON-only data never shows (64.0 to 60.0 and 66.0 to 52.0; SFTdecline recovers JavaScript), and on
live prompts whose expected value is a canonical form of what the user wrote (Chicago, IL where the user wrote
Chi-town). The effect-safety training is not the cause: in only 5 of the 122 lost prompts of the Python and live call
categories does the model answer with a question instead of a call. Finally, BFCL scores the form of a call, not whether
it should have been made with confirmation, whether it repeats an earlier call, or whether it writes over state the model
has not read; on those properties, which are what the effect declarations are for, the comparison is the one of Tables 3
and 4.
7
Limitations
EffectWorld is small: nine task kinds in one training domain, one test-only domain, and template utterances (paraphrases
generated by a larger language model are not yet used). The scripted user never changes its mind and confirms every
proposal it recognises as one, including a proposal to act on the wrong entity; the verifier counts any proposal the
scripted user confirmed as confirmed. A model that asks, is told the entity does not exist, and then proposes a substitute
is therefore scored as if it had not asked (Figure 6). Efficiency negatives are only as good as the optimizer’s provenance
heuristics; legality is checked by replay of the recorded trace, not proved for all states. BFCL multi-turn and τ 2-bench,
which test policies with a model-based user, are not yet evaluated end to end. The detailed analyses use one backbone,
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507; the other two model families of §4.3 are evaluated on the same benchmarks but analysed
less deeply, and all models have at most 4B parameters. The SFT models are from one training seed. The control,
unlikelihood and NPO variants were replicated with a second seed. The two control seeds differ by 1–3 points, which is
within noise (1.2 points on the hard split, 0.9 on commerce). Every unlikelihood conclusion holds within the second
seed, against that seed’s control: 3.5 points lower on the hard split, 16.8 lower on commerce and 8.3 lower on irrelevance
detection, with gains of 2.2 on live multiple and 2.6 on multi-turn. NPO is unstable across seeds. Its second seed scores
2.8 points higher than the first on the hard split, which brings it level with its control, but 8.2 points lower on commerce,
with 53 unconfirmed destructive calls against 16. The intervals in Tables 12 and 13 measure task sampling only.
8
Conclusion
Declaring what tools do makes efficiency and safety checkable properties of a trace. In EffectWorld the declarations
drive a replay verifier that decides effect safety without labels or judge models, an optimizer that proves calls unnecessary,
and held-out splits aimed at specific failures. Supervised fine-tuning on this data lifts a 4B model from 53% to 99%
task success in distribution, from 52% to 81% on unseen task kinds, and from 74% to 83% on an unseen shopping
domain, with about a third of the output tokens. We trained the same recipes on three model families, Qwen3, Phi-4 and
Falcon3, from 1.7B to 4B parameters: in each, SFT lifts in-distribution success to between 69% and 99% and adds 26
to 55 points on the held-out splits.
Three findings reach beyond our setup. First, public tool-use data can teach a small model to refuse: templated declines
in widely used corpora cut BFCL non-live accuracy from 88.0 to 31.4 while every in-domain metric looked perfect,
and only an external benchmark exposed it. Second, negatives did not substitute for positives. With identical positive

exposure, neither suppression nor DPO on negatives that are redundant or unsafe by construction beat SFT on any
held-out split; they mainly changed how readily the model calls. Third, positives transfer along the cue they show.
Declining when a tool is absent did not become declining when an offered tool cannot serve the request; positives with
exactly that cue restored BFCL irrelevance detection to the base model’s level and lifted the BFCL average above it, but
made the model skip confirmation on unseen tools. Confirming before destructive calls transferred to unseen tools in no
family: on the shopping domain the four base models make 1 to 7 unconfirmed destructive calls, their SFT adapters 6 to
64. Efficiency transfers to new tools; effect discipline stays tied to the tools it was learned on.
For practitioners this suggests three steps: declare effects, so that such failures can be measured; audit public data for
refusal templates before mixing it in; and write positive examples for every decision the agent must make, including
declining, asking and confirming, instead of relying on negatives to carve them out.
Several directions follow from what this study did not vary or could not yet test.
• Other adapters. Every model here uses rank-16 LoRA on the attention and MLP projections. Larger ranks,
adapters on all linear layers, or weight-decomposed adapters such as DoRA (Liu et al., 2024a) would show
whether the small adapter’s capacity is part of why SFT overwrites the base model’s calibration on irrelevance
prompts, and whether the negatives behave differently when the adapter can represent more.
• Full fine-tuning. LoRA is known to learn less and forget less than full fine-tuning (Biderman et al., 2024).
Training all weights on the same mixes would separate what the data teaches from what the adapter can hold,
and would test whether the loss of irrelevance detection grows or shrinks when nothing constrains the update.
• More complex tasks. EffectWorld’s tasks are short: optimal plans make about two calls, and every task stays
within one domain. Longer horizons, tasks that span domains, programs whose references carry computed
values rather than identifiers (the gap NESTful exposes), and tools that fail, time out and are retried (Li, 2026)
would test effect discipline where it matters most.
• Calling, asking and declining. SFTnear-miss supplies the near-miss cue as positive examples; When2Call (Ross
et al., 2025) found that a preference objective over the three options teaches this decision better than SFT, and
asking which of two same-named contacts is meant still needs positive examples of its own (Qian et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2025c).
• Confirmation tied to effects, not names. Renaming every tool in every trajectory, or adding a third training
domain, would separate the two; if that works, the declarations should be rendered by default, and the Model
Context Protocol annotations are the place to carry them.
• Reinforcement learning with the verifier as reward. The replay verifier scores any trajectory without a
judge, so it can serve directly as the reward for on-policy training (Qian et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a), and
an adapter’s own failures can supply negatives (Song et al., 2024).
• A richer user and larger models. A scripted user who declines or changes their mind, paraphrased requests,
and end-to-end evaluation on BFCL multi-turn and τ 2-bench (Barres et al., 2025) need a teacher model we
could not yet serve. The three families studied here stop at 4B parameters; repeating the study at 7B to 8B
would show whether the gap between efficiency and effect discipline narrows with scale.
