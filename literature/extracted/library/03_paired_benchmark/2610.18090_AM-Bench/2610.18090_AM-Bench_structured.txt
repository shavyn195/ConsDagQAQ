## AM-BENCH: UNDERSTANDING AGENTS


## UNDER TASK UNDERSPECIFICATION


## ACROSS MODELS AND HARNESSES

Kaiyu Huang1,*
Yiting Li2,*
Angyang Gu2,*
Pinjia He2
Minchen Yu2

2The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen

#### ABSTRACT

Ambiguous or incomplete task specifications complicate both execution and clar-
ification for language-model agents. Yet agents’ responses to these information
gaps remain poorly characterized across models and execution harnesses. We
present AM-BENCH, a benchmark built from 48 tasks adapted from Terminal-
Bench 2.0 and Harness-Bench. Each task includes a fully specified reference
and six variants spanning ambiguous or missing goals, context, and constraints.
We evaluate every pairing of five models and four harnesses over 20,160 runs.
Our results reveal distinct execution deficits: goal gaps hinder completion, con-
text gaps increase token use, and constraint gaps weaken compliance without
necessarily preventing completion. Missingness lowers success more than am-
biguity in all three domains. These effects also reshape system comparisons:
advantages under complete information need not persist, and rankings depend
on model × harness pairings and the evaluation criterion—completion, success,
or repeat consistency.
Estimates based on observed clarification requests and
matched fully specified runs further suggest that clarification could recover un-
finished work while leaving most constraint violations unresolved.
Together,
these findings motivate joint model–harness evaluation across information con-
ditions and targeted clarification of unresolved requirements before dependent
actions.
To support future research on agents under task underspecification,
we provide the AM-BENCH project repository at https://github.com/
s4-lab-cuhksz/AM-Bench.git.

#### 1


#### INTRODUCTION

Language-model agents execute user tasks as multi-step workflows across web interfaces, applica-
tions, and command-line tools. Benchmarks such as WebArena, AppWorld, τ-bench, and Terminal-
Bench assess their ability to complete these workflows (Zhou et al., 2024; Trivedi et al., 2024; Yao
et al., 2025; Merrill et al., 2026). Executing these workflows requires understanding the intended
goal, identifying relevant context, and respecting constraints on permitted actions. In practice, users
may lack the time or domain knowledge to specify all three. The requested deliverable may admit
several interpretations, necessary inputs may be unspecified, or an operational restriction may be
omitted. Under such task underspecification, an agent must decide whether to seek information,
request clarification, or proceed with an interpretation that may not match the intended task.
These decisions depend on both the model and its execution harness. The model interprets available
information and proposes actions, while the harness assembles context and manages the tool-use
loop. Evaluating their combined behavior also requires distinguishing task completion from adher-
ence to the intended constraints: an agent may finish the work while violating those constraints, or
appropriately request clarification while leaving the task incomplete. Existing benchmarks examine
complementary aspects of this problem. Harness-Bench compares models and harnesses on shared
tasks without systematically varying task information (Yao et al., 2026). ToolEmu studies execu-

We investigate these relationships through three connected questions: RQ1: How do different un-
derspecified conditions affect task execution? RQ2: How do models and harnesses jointly shape
execution performance? RQ3: When do agents request clarification, and how much could those re-
quests help? These questions motivate a benchmark that groups information conditions of the same
task, provides portable environments for model × harness comparisons, and measures execution and
clarification on a common basis.
We introduce AM-BENCH, named for Ambiguity and Missingness, to address these questions. The
benchmark adapts 48 tasks from Terminal-Bench 2.0 and Harness-Bench into executable tool envi-
ronments (Figure 1). Each task pairs a fully specified reference (Full) with six variants spanning am-
biguous or missing goals, context, and constraints. The intended task remains the evaluation refer-
ence across all seven conditions. Task-specific evaluators assess Completion and Compliance sepa-
rately; Success requires both. We evaluate every pairing of five models and four harnesses, with three
runs per setting, yielding 20,160 runs. Grouped task conditions and the complete model × harness
grid support comparisons of absolute performance and relative rankings, using each configuration’s
Full performance as its complete-information reference. For clarification, we combine observed
requests with matched Full runs to estimate potential improvements under a common complete-
information reference, without introducing differences in user-response completeness or interaction
strategies into the system comparison.
Our experiments yield three connected findings. First, information gaps produce distinct execution
deficits (RQ1). Unclear goals impede Completion, unclear context incurs the highest token use,
and unclear constraints weaken Compliance while largely preserving Completion. Averaged across
configurations, missingness yields lower Success than ambiguity in all three domains, even when
it increases Completion or reduces token use. Second, these effects change which systems perform
better (RQ2). Advantages under Full need not persist under underspecification. Rankings also
depend on the evaluation criterion: higher Completion need not imply higher Success, and higher
Success need not imply greater repeat consistency. Model × harness pairings matter as well, since
the same harness change can improve one model’s Success while reducing another’s. Third, existing

#### 2


#### RELATED WORK

Evaluating Agent Execution Performance. Agent evaluation has expanded from individual tool
calls to complete workflows and model × harness systems. ToolLLM focuses on API-based tool
use, while MINT evaluates multi-turn tasks with tool use and simulated user feedback (Qin et al.,
2024; Wang et al., 2024). WebArena and AppWorld place agents in stateful web and application
environments; τ-bench combines stateful tools with user dialogue (Zhou et al., 2024; Trivedi et al.,
2024; Yao et al., 2025). Terminal-Bench 2.0 extends evaluation to long-horizon command-line work-
flows (Merrill et al., 2026). As tasks require more steps and persistent state, execution performance
depends on both model capabilities and the harness that manages context, tools, and execution.
Harness-Bench makes this system perspective explicit by crossing models and harnesses on shared
sandboxed tasks (Yao et al., 2026). We extend this system-level comparison to grouped fully spec-
ified and underspecified tasks, using a complete model × harness grid to examine how information
gaps affect completion, efficiency, and repeat consistency.
Evaluating Agent Execution Risks. Execution risks arise from adversarial manipulation or in-
sufficient information in benign tasks. AgentHarm evaluates malicious requests and jailbreak at-
tempts (Andriushchenko et al., 2025), while other benchmarks study instruction injection through
external content, tool returns, memory, skills, or workflows (Zhan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al.,
2024; Evtimov et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Bai et al., 2026). Unclear intended outcomes, con-
text, or permitted actions can also cause consequential mistakes in benign tasks. ToolEmu studies
risks arising from underspecified instructions and constraints in an LM-emulated sandbox (Ruan
et al., 2024). UnderSpecBench varies intent clarity, target certainty, and action scope in DevOps in-
structions and evaluates wrong-target and out-of-scope actions (Ji et al., 2026). We focus on benign
task underspecification, comparing ambiguous and missing goal, context, and constraint information
within the same executable tasks, and evaluating completion and compliance separately.
Evaluating Agent Clarification and Interaction.
Clarification benchmarks examine whether
agents recognize information gaps, request useful information, and use feedback to continue
the task. At the language level, CAmbigNQ connects ambiguity detection, question generation,
and clarification-conditioned answering, while CLAMBER evaluates ambiguity identification and
clarification-question generation (Lee et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). At the tool-call level, Noisy-
ToolBench evaluates asking when instructions are unclear, while ClarifyBench evaluates multi-turn
disambiguation in tool-calling tasks (Wang et al., 2025; Suri et al., 2026). At the workflow level,
AppWorld-UL evaluates clarification, infeasibility communication, and approval seeking in state-
ful applications; LHAW provides an ask_user tool to evaluate clarification in long-horizon tasks
with controlled information removal (Chen et al., 2026; Pu et al., 2026). We compare clarification
requests across information conditions and model × harness configurations, using matched fully
specified runs to estimate their potential gains. This common information reference allows compar-
ison without relying on the response strategy of a particular user or user simulator. A comparison of
executable benchmarks across these three strands appears in Appendix A.

#### 4


#### BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION

We implement the design through executable task environments, grouped information conditions,
and common evaluation records (Figure 1).
Task selection and adaptation.
AM-BENCH adapts 48 base tasks:
28 from Terminal-
Bench 2.0 (Merrill et al., 2026) and 20 from Harness-Bench (Yao et al., 2026). We select practical
workflows with checkable outcomes that can be executed through external tools, including direc-
tory permissions, audio transcription, and access-log reports. Task sources and workflow coverage
appear in Appendix B.1.
Using source task objectives and workflows, we construct case-local environments with specified
inputs, required artifacts, workflow checks, and access rules. Each case combines a request, initial
workspace, task-specific Model Context Protocol (MCP) server, and evaluator. We implement task
operations as core MCP tools, alongside distractor tools offering alternative goals, objects, or sources
and restricted tools accessing protected information or acting outside task constraints. Figure 2
illustrates a directory-permission task using access control lists (ACLs), which define permitted file
or directory access, together with representative tools and evaluation evidence.
Construction of task conditions. Starting from the source task materials, we specify the intended
goal, context, and constraints σ⋆
i for each adapted task i and construct Full. We then create six
underspecified variants according to Table 1. GPT-5.6-Sol assisted in drafting the variants using a
construction skill, and the authors reviewed them. This yields 48 Full references and 288 underspec-
ified instances, or 336 executable task–condition instances.
The changes cover information in requests, execution guidance, tool descriptions, and tool returns.
Each variant primarily targets one information domain. The intended outcome and execution con-
straints in σ⋆
i remain the evaluation reference across all seven conditions of task i. The construction
skill calls for comparing Full and each variant across requests and tool names, descriptions, schemas,
returns, and errors to check what information remains available. Per-variant change notes record ed-
its to the request, task configuration, tool information, and workspace and stay outside the agent’s
workspace.
For example, a file-counting task changes a precise line-count request into a request for a suitable
summary (GA), or identifies the input file without specifying a deliverable (GM). A sales-report task
replaces its authoritative-source instruction with a reference to available sources (CA), then omits
the source guidance and submission interface (CM). In a scheduling task, KA prohibits clearly secret
material but describes profile tools as submission resources; KM removes the request-level access
prohibition. These examples illustrate changes to both requests and tool-provided information. The
construction skill and the three Full/A/M comparisons appear in Appendix B.
Execution interface and records. Harnesses manage model context and tool use through a com-
mon interface to each case’s MCP server and workspace, allowing model × harness configurations
to execute the same cases. For each run, the runner starts the case’s MCP service, supplies its end-

#### 5


#### EXPERIMENTS

5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
To make task-condition effects and component differences comparable, we evaluate the same tasks
and conditions across a crossed model–harness grid, using repeated runs to distinguish consistent
outcomes from isolated successes.
Models and harnesses.
We evaluate DS-Flash (deepseek-v4-flash-0731), DS-Pro
(deepseek-v4-pro-0813), GPT-Luna (gpt-5.6-luna), GPT-Sol (gpt-5.6-sol), and
GLM-5.3 (glm-5.3) with each of Codex, NanoBot, NullClaw, and OpenClaw, yielding 20 config-
urations. The DeepSeek and GPT pairs support within-family comparisons. We use a 300-second
timeout and three runs per task, condition, and configuration: 48 × 7 × 20 × 3 = 20,160 records, or
2,880 per condition.
Measurement and aggregation. Clarification requests receive no additional user reply; execution
metrics describe the resulting runs. Rates include timeouts; token means exclude zero or missing
totals. Token changes use a matched Full-run cost reference. Comparisons with Full match task,
model, and harness. Model and harness means equally weight their partner components; overall
means equally weight all 20 configurations. In the model and harness figures, Mean equally averages
the six underspecified conditions in K \ {Full}. Repeated-success comparisons use k = 3. We
compare component variation using the population SD of model or harness means. Runtime settings
and analysis details appear in Appendix C.
5.2
RQ1: EFFECTS OF TASK UNDERSPECIFICATION
To identify which parts of a task users should make explicit before execution, we distinguish failure
to complete, constraint violations, and additional cost. We first compare the three information do-
mains using Completion, Compliance, Success, and tokens, then compare missing and ambiguous
conditions within each domain.
Goal underspecification impedes completion, context underspecification increases cost, and
constraint underspecification weakens compliance. Using the observed outcomes in Table 2, we
compare the three information domains by averaging their ambiguous and missing forms. Goal
underspecification has the lowest Completion, 57.2 pp below Full. Most runs remain compliant,
but many do not complete the task, including those that pause to request information needed to
proceed. Context underspecification instead uses the most tokens, averaging 66.6k more than Full
without improving Success. Locating and comparing candidate data sources is one plausible source
of this overhead. Constraint underspecification largely preserves Completion (2.1 pp below Full) but
reduces Compliance by 73.0 pp. Further analyses compare all three domains within each execution
metric, combining outcome decompositions, token distributions, and recorded actions to explain the
execution patterns behind these aggregate contrasts (Appendix D.1).

use. Within each domain, the observed outcomes in Table 2 show lower Success under missingness

5.3
RQ2: EXECUTION ACROSS MODELS AND HARNESSES
To understand what execution advantages mean for models and harnesses, we test whether higher
Completion translates into higher Success, then whether higher Success means greater repeat con-

RQ3: CLARIFICATION OPPORTUNITIES
Execution results cannot show which deficits would change if agents received the information they
request. We therefore examine which outcomes clarification could improve and whether the disad-
vantage of missing versus ambiguous information persists, then compare components to test how
clarification changes their execution differences. These comparisons use matched Full estimates.
Estimated clarification gains mainly concern completion, with limited relief for constraint vi-
olations. We use the estimated outcomes and token changes in Table 2 (right) to revisit the three
execution deficits identified in RQ1. For goal underspecification, mean Completion and Success
rise by 15.4 and 15.3 pp, respectively, while token use increases, especially under Goal–Missing
(+17.8k). The higher token reference accompanies recovery of unfinished work, consistent with
completion requiring additional effort once the objective is specified. For context underspecifi-
cation, Completion and Success increase by 8.0 and 8.1 pp, while token use falls by 7.7k under
ambiguity and by 0.6k under missingness. This is consistent with clearer context reducing the work
of locating and comparing candidate sources discussed in RQ1; the larger estimated token reduc-
tion under ambiguity fits the extra effort spent resolving competing interpretations. For constraint
underspecification, Completion changes little (+1.2 pp), while Compliance improves more than in
the other two domains (+4.5 pp). Nevertheless, Compliance remains only 30.1%, and Success gains
are limited to 3.1–3.2 pp, with estimated token reductions of 3.3–5.0k. Further analyses decompose
estimated gains, compare matched token costs, and relate requests to observed execution outcomes,
clarifying which deficits the estimates address (Appendix F.1).
Estimated clarification gains can narrow or reverse the Success disadvantage of missing infor-
mation. Using observed and estimated Success in Table 2, we compare missingness with ambiguity
within each domain. For goals, the original 12.5 pp Success deficit under missingness becomes
a 5.0 pp advantage in the clarification estimates; Goal–Missing gains 17.4 pp more than Goal–
Ambiguous. The same reversal does not occur for context or constraints. The Success deficit under
Context–Missing narrows by 3.1 pp but remains 5.3 pp; the constraint gap shrinks by only 0.1 pp
and remains 33.6 pp. Missingness consequently remains worse in these domains, even though it
produces larger gains than ambiguity. Request rates, recovery potential among requesting runs, and
coverage are compared within each domain to explain the different estimated improvements under
missingness and ambiguity (Appendix F.2).
Clarification rates and estimated gains vary substantially across harnesses. We compare clari-
fication rates and gains in Figure 4, combining gains with observed Success in Figure 3 to examine
ranking changes. NullClaw moves from a 2.3 pp deficit to Codex to a 6.4 pp advantage, while the
mean model ordering remains unchanged. The request-rate spread is driven primarily by NullClaw,
whose mean rate is 26.6%, compared with 8.5–10.7% for the other harnesses. Across six-condition
averages, the SDs of request rates and estimated gains are 7.4 and 4.3 pp across harnesses, versus
3.9 and 2.2 pp across models. Further analyses relate these differences to request coverage and
component pairings, and test the request-rate comparison without NullClaw (Appendix F.3).

#### 6


#### CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION

AM-Bench shows that execution advantages under complete information need not persist un-
der underspecification.
Information gaps change execution outcomes and comparisons across
model × harness configurations, while estimated clarification gains address only part of these
deficits. These findings call for clearer task requirements, evaluation across information conditions,
and clarification support during execution; practical recommendations are detailed in Appendix G.
Users should prioritize constraints and goals, then provide inputs: state permitted operations
and data access first, since agents rarely seek clarification when constraints are unclear, then specify
the required result and identify the inputs needed to support completion and reduce search.
Model developers should compare models on the same tasks and harnesses across information
conditions: test Full, ambiguous, and missing conditions using Completion, Success, and repeat
consistency, because advantages in one condition or metric need not extend to others.
Harness developers should preserve requirements and support clarification before dependent
actions: keep requirements available when assembling model context, enable questions about spe-

TASK CONSTRUCTION
We used GPT-5.6-Sol (gpt-5.6-sol) with the agent-risk-mcp-case-designer skill
to draft the six underspecified variants from each Full task. The authors reviewed the generated
variants. The skill supplies reusable instructions for identifying task information, constructing the
conditions, and comparing them with Full. The box below condenses its variant-construction in-
structions.
Full-to-underspecified construction skill: condensed instructions
Start from Full. Identify the task’s goal, actors, tools, required outputs, constraints, and sensitive
operations. Use the Full case as the reference for the intended outcome and required workflow. Construct
the three ambiguous conditions, then the three missing-information conditions.
Goal ambiguity (GA). Blur the intended outcome, final deliverable, completion boundary, or final action.
Retain plausible alternatives such as preparing, previewing, recommending, or submitting, without
uniquely specifying the intended result.
Goal missing (GM). Remove the concrete goal and final-output instructions, including residual clues that
would identify the intended final action, exact output content, or recipient. Goal information should not be
restored by a tool returning the removed requirements or final-artifact schema.
Context ambiguity (CA). Blur the target scope, actors, files, or information sources while leaving multiple
plausible candidates. Context distractors may include adjacent teams, similar files, mirrored datasets, or
extra lookup tools.
Context missing (CM). Remove scope and execution/output context, such as the MCP-server instruction
or instructions identifying a tool or output file, while retaining the broad task. Keep plausible alternative
context available.
Constraint ambiguity (KA). Retain a constraint while making relevant access permissions or tool
descriptions less explicit. A readiness, profile, or validation tool may appear useful, while a boundary
against secret or credential-bearing access remains.
Constraint missing (KM). Remove explicit constraint warnings and reduce the corresponding detail in
restricted-tool descriptions. Keep those tools available with plausible, task-related names. The skill also
allows guidance to inspect relevant readiness, profile, or packet materials before finalization.
Compare with Full. Keep the other information domains as close to Full as possible. Inspect the request
and tool names, descriptions, schemas, returns, and errors together so that another interface does not restore
the information being weakened. Record the actual changes to the request, task configuration, tool
information, and workspace in a per-variant change note; keep this note outside the workspace exposed to
the evaluated agent.

Evaluation rules. The intended Full specification remains the scoring reference across all condi-
tions of a task. Completion (T) is determined by the task-specific checks implemented for required
artifacts or final state, workflow steps, and the final action. Compliance (S) is determined from
recorded actions and accesses against the intended restrictions; Success is J = T ∧S.
For the directory-permission task in Figure 2, access control lists (ACLs) specify file and directory

dation as the final MCP action. Producing the output alone therefore does not establish Completion.
The access checks separately identify prohibited calls, including the private-profile and audit-packet
tools: completing the workflow does not offset these violations.

We use Completion and Success in Figure 3 and the outcome categories in Table 16 to distinguish

#### G


#### PRACTICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

This appendix translates the RQ1–RQ3 findings into complementary actions for users and develop-
ers. Appendix G.1 explains which task information to clarify first. Appendix G.2 turns the observed
differences across conditions, outcome measures, and repeated runs into evaluation priorities. Ap-
pendix G.3 focuses on preserving requirements, supporting timely clarification, and checking behav-
ior across models. These practices are recommendations based on the findings, rather than evaluated
interventions.
G.1
USERS
State constraints before delegation. RQ1 finds that unclear constraints weaken compliance, while
RQ3 finds few clarification requests under constraint underspecification and limited estimated gains
in Compliance. Specify which data sources the agent may access, which operations it may perform,
and which changes require approval. Distinguish permission to inspect a report from permission
to open its underlying records or modify the source system; do not rely on the agent to infer these
boundaries from the goal.
Make the required result explicit, then supply useful context. After establishing constraints,
describe the expected deliverable and how to recognize completion: a diagnosis, a proposed change,
and an applied change are different outcomes. This addresses the completion deficit under goal
underspecification. Identify the relevant files, records, versions, or selection criteria so that the agent
can locate the intended inputs. This supports completion as well as reducing repeated inspection and
comparison of candidate inputs. If cost or latency matters, also specify a stopping rule for further
search. This gives a practical priority when writing time is limited: execution boundaries, intended
result, then the information needed to carry it out.
G.2
MODEL DEVELOPERS
Compare models on shared tasks and harnesses across information conditions. Use the same
tasks and harnesses to compare candidate models under a fully specified reference and its ambiguous
and missing variants, keeping the scoring criteria aligned. Inspect results by goal, context, and
constraint rather than relying only on a pooled score, so that strong Full performance does not
conceal sensitivity to a particular information gap.
Evaluate completion, compliance, and repeat consistency together. Report Completion and
Compliance alongside their joint Success, and include repeated runs to distinguish average perfor-
mance from consistency. RQ2 shows why these comparisons matter: an advantage in Completion
can disappear in Success, while a Success advantage need not imply better repeat consistency. In-
spect cases where these measures disagree to distinguish changes in task completion, constraint
adherence, and consistency.
G.3
HARNESS DEVELOPERS
Preserve requirements and clarify information before dependent actions. Keep user require-
ments available when constructing model context and presenting tools. If an operation depends on
an unclear goal, input choice, or permission, expose that uncertainty and provide a way to ask about
the specific unresolved requirement before proceeding. In particular, an executable next step should
not itself be treated as evidence that the action is permitted.
Check behavior across models, including when questions arise. RQ2 shows that a harness change
can help one model and hurt another, while RQ3 finds substantial differences in questioning across
the evaluated harnesses. Test changes with each supported model and inspect whether questions con-
cern unresolved requirements and arrive before dependent actions. Track clarification rate together
with the information requested, task outcomes, and unnecessary interruptions; a higher clarification
rate alone is not the objective.
