## LangNCO: Compiling Natural-Language Constraints into Inference-Time


## Interventions for Neural Combinatorial Optimization


### Hironori Ohigashi

Panasonic Connect Co., Ltd.
ohigashi.hironori@jp.panasonic.com
Abstract
Neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) rapidly produces
high-quality solutions for problem structures encountered dur-
ing training, but operational requirements may change af-
ter deployment and be communicated in natural language.
We propose LangNCO, a framework that compiles instance-
specific requirements into structured inference-time interven-
tions for a frozen NCO solver, without changing the pre-
trained policy’s architecture, learned input representation, or
parameters. A pretrained LLM serves as a replaceable se-
mantic front end, translating each requirement into a schema-
validated domain-specific language (DSL) rule set. A deter-
ministic Rule Executor applies the rules as nonexpansive hard
action masks. Because local masking prevents immediate vi-
olations but does not ensure that a complete feasible contin-
uation remains reachable, Critical-Offset Decoding extracts
rule-referenced critical nodes and reallocates part of a fixed
rollout budget to multi-strength biased rollouts. Using con-
trolled task-specific language to isolate solver adaptation from
open-ended language robustness, we evaluate a CVRP policy
on road closures, time windows, vehicle assignments, visit-
order requirements, all pairwise combinations, and twelve
additional constraint types. LangNCO achieves 100% fea-
sibility on the four primary datasets with mean reference
gaps of 1.63–3.26%, 97.33–100% feasibility when separately
generated rules are composed for pairwise constraints, and
98.67% feasibility across the additional constraints. These re-
sults demonstrate modular post-training adaptation of a frozen
NCO solver through inspectable language-derived rules, with-
out retraining.

### 1


### Introduction

Combinatorial optimization underlies routing, scheduling,
production planning, and robotic decision making. In these
domains, high-quality discrete decisions often must be pro-
duced repeatedly under tight time limits. Neural combinato-
rial optimization (NCO) learns models that accelerate solu-
tion generation for such problems on new instances (Bello
et al. 2017; Bengio, Lodi, and Prouvost 2021). A central
line of NCO research focuses on constructive policies that
directly build complete solutions under problem structures
specified during training, enabling fast inference after train-
ing (Vinyals, Fortunato, and Jaitly 2015; Nazari et al. 2018;
Kool, van Hoof, and Welling 2019; Kwon et al. 2020). In
routing, these policies typically construct routes autoregres-
sively by repeatedly selecting the next customer.
Operational requirements, however, often change after de-
ployment. Roads may close, customer time requirements
may change, or temporary vehicle-assignment and visit-order
rules may be introduced. Natural language provides a flexible
interface for specifying such instance-specific requirements,
but a standard pretrained solver cannot interpret constraints
absent from its training-time problem definition. Retraining
the policy, adding constraint-specific inputs, or redesigning
the decoder for every transient requirement is impractical.
We study inference-time adaptation to state-action-level
constraints, whose effects can be evaluated during decoding
from the current partial solution, candidate attributes, and
exposed environment state. This class covers restrictions in-
volving transitions, time, vehicle identity, visit history, capac-
ity, depot access, and other state-dependent conditions. Con-
straints requiring evaluation only after all routes are complete
are outside this scope. Starting from a policy trained only on
the standard capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP),
our goal is to incorporate instance-specific natural-language
requirements without constraint-specific policy inputs, archi-
tecture changes, or weight updates.
Recent multi-task and unified NCO systems generalize
across routing variants through shared architectures and at-
tribute representations (Liu et al. 2024a; Zhou et al. 2024;
Berto et al. 2025b; Li et al. 2025; Goh et al. 2025; Zhou
et al. 2026). Their zero-shot settings nevertheless assume
that the relevant attributes, state interfaces, and feasibility
mechanisms are defined before training; the unseen element
is typically a problem variant or a combination of supported
attributes within that interface. We instead address post-
training requirements first specified in natural language for
a concrete instance. LLM-based optimization methods have
also generated solutions, mathematical formulations, solver
programs, and heuristics (Yang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024b;
Ahmaditeshnizi, Gao, and Udell 2024; Jiang et al. 2025b,a;
Romera-Paredes et al. 2024). Rather than assigning combina-
torial search to the LLM or reconstructing the base problem
through a separate solver pipeline, we retain the pretrained
NCO policy as the search engine and use the LLM only to
specify state-dependent action interventions.
To realize this division of labor, we propose LangNCO,
an inference-time framework in which an LLM, serving as
a replaceable semantic front end, translates each require-
ment into a compact, schema-validatable domain-specific

language (DSL) that specifies state-dependent changes to
the feasible action set. The validated rules are deterministi-
cally compiled, and a Rule Executor applies them as action
masks over the frozen decoder’s base feasibility mask. The
NCO policy remains responsible for route construction and
cost minimization. This design preserves the routing pol-
icy’s inductive bias and fast inference, makes interventions
inspectable, and avoids free-form tensor code generation.
Hard masking ensures immediate action legality but not
completion viability: a legal choice may consume capacity
needed by an assigned customer, delay a time-window cus-
tomer beyond recovery, or close a route before a prerequisite
is served. We therefore introduce Critical-Offset Decoding,
which extracts explicitly referenced critical nodes from the
generated rules and reallocates part of a fixed rollout budget
to multi-strength biased rollouts. It never revives a masked
action and balances completion-oriented exploration with the
original policy’s cost-oriented search.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate post-
training request adaptation of a fixed CVRP policy and intro-
duce a modular language-to-rule interface whose validated
output drives deterministic, nonexpansive action interven-
tion, without adding constraint-specific inputs to the NCO
policy or changing its architecture or weights. Second, we
propose Critical-Offset Decoding, which biases a subset of
rollouts toward rule-derived critical nodes, reducing future
deadlocks and improving solution quality without increas-
ing the rollout budget. Third, we evaluate the framework on
four primary constraint types, all pairwise combinations, and
twelve additional types. The complete method attains 100%
feasibility on all four primary datasets with mean reference
gaps of 1.63–3.26%, 97.33–100% feasibility when separately
generated rules are composed for pairwise constraints, and
98.67% feasibility across the additional constraints. These
results show that structured language-derived rules can ex-
tend a frozen NCO solver beyond its training-time problem
definition without retraining.

### 2


### Related Work

NCO and generalization.
Constructive NCO policies
build solutions sequentially. Pointer Networks introduced
attention-based permutation generation, while subsequent
reinforcement-learning methods learned routing policies
without optimal labels; the Attention Model and POMO
further established Transformer-style encoders, feasibility
masking, and multistart decoding (Vinyals, Fortunato, and
Jaitly 2015; Bello et al. 2017; Nazari et al. 2018; Kool, van
Hoof, and Welling 2019; Kwon et al. 2020). Later work
improved scalability and decoder capacity and systematized
NCO evaluation (Jin et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2023; Bengio,
Lodi, and Prouvost 2021; Berto et al. 2025a). Generalist
solvers instead share representations across routing prob-
lems, constraint combinations, and distributions (Liu et al.
2024a; Zhou et al. 2024; Berto et al. 2025b; Li et al. 2025;
Goh et al. 2025); URS extends this direction with unified
representations and model-agnostic feasibility masks for un-
seen routing variants (Zhou et al. 2026), while other work
studies distributional and size generalization (Jiang et al.
2022; Zhou et al. 2023). These methods generalize within
structured solver interfaces whose attributes and feasibility
mechanisms are specified before training. In contrast, we
freeze a policy trained only on standard CVRP and compose
concrete target entities, state guards, and action effects from
natural language at inference time. Our setting is therefore
post-training request adaptation rather than cross-problem
generalization of a pretrained generalist.
LLMs for combinatorial optimization.
LLMs have been
used to propose complete or intermediate solutions (Yang
et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024b; Iklassov et al. 2024; Jiang
et al. 2025a), translate natural-language descriptions into
optimization formulations or solver programs (Ahmaditesh-
nizi, Gao, and Udell 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Jiang et al.
2025b; Astorga et al. 2025; Ramamonjison et al. 2022), and
search for reusable algorithms or heuristics (Romera-Paredes
et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2024). These approaches either assign
substantial combinatorial search to the LLM or construct a
separate optimization pipeline. We instead generate only a
compact action-intervention rule and retain the pretrained
NCO policy as the route-construction and search compo-
nent. The base CVRP formulation remains unchanged, while
the request modifies only the admissibility or priority of af-
fected actions. This separation keeps semantic interpretation
distinct from combinatorial search for a low-cost feasible
solution.
Constraint-aware decoding and structured intervention.
Action masks are standard in autoregressive routing, and
related state-specific masks and safety layers suppress in-
valid or unsafe actions (Wang et al. 2024; Krasowski et al.
2023). Proactive Infeasibility Prevention masks actions using
constraint-specific lookahead, while LazyMask combines
lookahead with backtracking (Bi et al. 2024; Li et al. 2026).
Our intervention is instead derived from an instance-specific
language-generated rule and applied to a frozen standard-
CVRP policy. The same representation provides both a de-
terministic hard mask for local legality and a structural signal
that directs inference-time search toward critical nodes.
Constrained generation methods such as PICARD,
grammar-constrained decoding, and grammar prompting im-
prove formal validity (Scholak, Schucher, and Bahdanau
2021; Geng et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023), whereas code-
generation studies show that syntactic validity does not pre-
vent failures in API use, data shapes, or executable semantics
(Chen et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022; Lai et al. 2023; Ni et al.
2024). Our DSL therefore restricts generation to guarded tar-
get/effect rules, while parsing, schema validation, compila-
tion, and validation-feedback retries fix tensor operations,
shape handling, and mask-composition semantics outside
the LLM. The resulting rules remain inspectable, and their
structured fields are reused for decoding bias. Our contri-
bution is thus a shared intermediate representation connect-
ing natural-language interpretation, deterministic action in-
tervention, and inference-time search guidance for a fixed
learned optimizer.

#### State–Action Rules

Rather than generating a final route or free-form code, the
LLM translates each requirement into one or more instance-
specific state–action rules in a JSON DSL. Each rule specifies
candidate targets, optional state- and candidate-level condi-
tions, and one of two nonexpansive effects: blocking matched
actions or retaining only them.
Targets may denote the depot, all customers, fixed node
IDs, or attribute-defined customer sets. Conditions may ref-
erence the current node, cumulative or vehicle-specific visit
history, vehicle identity, capacity, route length, demand, coor-
dinates, and auxiliary observations exposed by the simulator.
The DSL fixes typed operators and state-access forms
while allowing an extensible observation namespace. At in-
ference time, the simulator may expose instance-specific at-
tributes or pairwise matrices, such as elapsed time or travel
times. These observations are used only by the Rule Executor
and are not added to the pretrained NCO policy input.
Rather than selecting from predefined VRP-specific rules
or DSL templates, the system composes targets, constants,
guards, and effects from shared primitives and available ob-
servations. A request is supported when its required state
quantities are exposed. Restricting generation to system-
defined operations keeps execution semantics under compiler
control.

level condition, and hc(st, a) its candidate-level condition.

Validation and compilation.
After JSON parsing, the gen-
erated rule set is validated against the DSL schema and
checked by compilation using the same compiler that gen-
erates the executable rules. Schema validation checks the
rule-set structure and schema-defined expression forms. The
compile check rejects expressions that cannot be translated
under the supported DSL semantics, including unsupported
operators, access patterns, and node-set forms, as well as
candidate-indexed references used outside a candidate con-
text.
For each valid global or instance-scoped collection of
constraints, the compiler emits a self-contained PyTorch
mask function. It translates target sets into batched candi-
date masks, state-level conditions into row-wise expressions,
and candidate-level conditions into node-wise expressions.
Where needed, the compiler reconciles original-unit quan-
tities in the DSL with the normalized solver state and fixes
state access, broadcasting, tensor-shape handling, and non-
expansive mask composition.
If JSON parsing, schema validation, or compilation fails,
the resulting error is returned to the LLM for at most two
repair attempts. These checks ensure that a rule is schema-
conformant and executable under the supported DSL, but
do not verify semantic agreement with the natural-language
requirement. Additional details on the typed rule represen-
tation, validation procedure, and compilation semantics are
provided in the Technical Supplement.

#### Critical-Offset Decoding

From local legality to completion-oriented search.
Hard
masking guarantees only local legality with respect to the ex-
ecuted base and DSL constraints: an allowed action may still
eliminate all complete continuations. For example, delaying
a time-window customer may make its deadline unreachable,
another customer may consume the capacity needed by an as-
signed customer, or a route may terminate before its ordering
requirements are completed.
Exactly determining whether each action preserves a com-
plete continuation would require constraint-specific looka-
head or solving a constrained completion problem for every
candidate. Critical-Offset Decoding instead derives a low-
cost, completion-oriented search prior from the structure of
the generated DSL.
Many such nonlocal failures are associated with nodes
referenced by the generated constraints. A customer directly
restricted by a constraint may lose its service opportunity if it
is postponed until a deadline expires, the designated vehicle
loses capacity, or the current route terminates. Likewise, the
state of a node referenced by a constraint condition may
determine whether another customer becomes or remains
available.

### Experiments


#### Experimental Setup

Base solver and data.
We use RouteFinder (Berto et al.
2025b) trained only on standard CVRP. The base test set
contains 300 instances from the LLMCoSolver benchmark
(Jiang et al. 2025a), with 10–100 customer nodes and LKH-3
near-optimal routes supplied for the unconstrained instances.
Decoding uses POMO-style multistarts and eight geometric
augmentations. The RouteFinder architecture and parameters
remain fixed in all experiments.
Constraint-augmented datasets.
We construct four pri-
mary datasets by adding one hard constraint to each base
instance and selecting its targets so that the original high-
quality solution violates the new requirement. Road closes
an adjacent non-depot edge in both directions. Time imposes
a no-wait hard time window of width 18–20 on an early-route
customer, using travel time ⌈0.1 × Euclidean distance⌉. Ve-
hicle assigns two capacity-compatible, high-demand cus-
tomers, selected from different reference routes when pos-
sible, to the same vehicle. Order requires two prerequisite
nodes to precede a target node on the same route, using a
sandwich construction such that neither the original route
nor its reversal satisfies the precedence constraint. Every re-
tained primary instance has a constraint-feasible reference
solution.
The constraints are expressed using controlled, task-
specific natural-language descriptions with instance-specific

--- PAGE 6 ---

entities and values. Our evaluation focuses on constraint-type
and compositional breadth rather than systematic robustness
to paraphrasing.
Methods and metrics.
LLMCoSolver and Direct GPT-
5 serve as direct-solution baselines that generate com-
plete routes from the problem description. NCO w/o In-
tervention is the unmodified pretrained RouteFinder de-
coder; it receives neither the natural-language constraint
nor a derived rule mask. Our variants use either Gemma4
(gemma4:31b-it-q8_0) or GPT-5 only to generate the
DSL, after which both use the same Rule Executor and
Critical-Offset Decoding.
DSL generation uses an initial attempt and up to two
validation-feedback retries after parsing, schema-validation,
or compile-check failures. If no valid rule is obtained, the
solver proceeds without an additional rule. After inference,
an independently implemented evaluation-only constraint
checker determines whether each returned route satisfies the
base CVRP constraints and all applicable requirements in the
natural-language specification. All feasibility rates reported
throughout the experiments are computed using this post-hoc
checker. The checker is not exposed to the inference pipeline
and cannot affect rule generation, validation-feedback retries,
action masking, decoding, completion filtering, or cost-based
rollout selection. Unparseable route outputs, runtime failures,
and decoding deadlocks count as infeasible. For feasible out-
puts, the reference gap is (C−Cref)/Cref×100. Primary-task
references use LKH-3 for Road (Helsgaun 2017) and OR-
Tools for Time, Vehicle, and Order (Google 2026); pairwise
references also use OR-Tools. None provides an optimality
certificate.
The Technical Supplement provides additional implemen-
tation details, model versions and inference settings, solver
configurations and time limits, random seeds, hardware de-
tails, and complete experimental results.

#### End-to-End Results

Table 1 evaluates the complete language-to-route pipeline.
Both proposed variants achieve 100% feasibility on all four
datasets, with reference gaps of 1.63–3.26%. In contrast,
NCO without intervention retains low route costs only on
the few instances that accidentally satisfy the new constraint,
reaching just 1.00–37.33% feasibility. Direct GPT-5 more of-
ten satisfies the constraints but produces substantially worse
routes, with gaps of 32.04–120.01%. LLMCoSolver reaches
at most 13.67% feasibility on three of the four unseen con-
straint types. These results support assigning language in-
terpretation to the LLM while retaining the pretrained NCO
policy as the combinatorial search engine.
Rule-representation robustness.
As an auxiliary compar-
ison, we replace the DSL with free-form Python rule gener-
ation while keeping the downstream solver and decoding
strategy fixed. The DSL achieves 100% feasibility in all
eight generator–constraint settings. Python generation falls
below 100% in three settings, with a minimum feasibility of
89.33%. Observed failures include tensor-shape mismatches,
inconsistent device placement, and indexing operations that
modify a copy rather than the original action mask. This aux-
iliary comparison indicates that restricting rule generation to
the DSL can mitigate low-level implementation failures ob-
served with free-form Python. A per-task breakdown and
representative generated-code failures are included in the
Technical Supplement.

#### Effectiveness of Critical-Offset Decoding

To isolate rollout guidance from errors in generated hard
masks, we replace the generated masks with independently
implemented reference masks while retaining DSL-derived
critical-node extraction. Critical nodes are still extracted
from the DSL generated by LangNCO-Gemma4 and are
not supplied through dataset labels. The greedy comparison
holds the original greedy decoding fixed and compares one
additional ordinary sample with one additional offset-guided
rollout. For sampling, both standard sampling and Critical-
Offset Decoding use the same budget of 100 rollouts per
POMO start and augmentation.
The greedy control shows that the gain cannot be explained
by adding one more rollout alone. On Vehicle, adding
one ordinary sample raises feasibility only from 75.33% to
77.33%, whereas replacing that additional rollout with an
offset-guided rollout raises it to 100%. On Order, the cor-
responding feasibility values are 83.67% and 100%.
Under the same budget of 100 rollouts per POMO start and
augmentation, standard sampling fails to produce feasible so-
lutions on 0.33%, 12.33%, and 3.00% of the Time, Vehicle,
and Order instances, respectively, whereas Critical-Offset
Decoding reaches 100% feasibility on all four datasets. The
reported mean gaps are also lower, changing from 2.19%,
3.33%, and 3.52% to 1.89%, 2.53%, and 2.85%, respectively.
For this matched-budget comparison, the mean decoder time
is nearly unchanged, increasing from 173.78 to 174.68 ms;
language generation and rule compilation are excluded from
this measurement.
These results reflect the distinction between local legal-
ity and completion viability described in Section 3. Ordi-
nary sampling may postpone a currently feasible but low-
probability critical node until a deadline expires, the desig-
nated vehicle’s capacity is consumed, or the current route is
closed. Offset-guided rollouts add trajectories that serve such
nodes while the opportunity remains available. On Road, the
negligible change is expected because the hard mask already
captures the immediate violation.

Additional constraint types.
We further evaluate twelve

correct rule that restricts customer arrivals without constrain-

limitations from semantic rule-generation errors.

### 5


### Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced LangNCO, a framework for adapting a frozen
NCO solver to instance-specific natural-language constraints
at inference time. An LLM compiles each requirement into
a schema-validated DSL rule, which a deterministic Rule
Executor applies as an action mask. Critical-Offset Decod-
ing further uses rule-derived critical nodes to mitigate future
deadlocks within a fixed rollout budget. In the controlled de-
coding evaluation with independently implemented reference
rule masks, it achieved 100% feasibility on all four primary
constraints under the same budget of 100 rollouts per POMO
start and augmentation, compared with 87.67–100% for stan-
dard sampling, while yielding lower mean reference gaps
on the three nonlocal constraints. The complete LangNCO
pipeline achieved 100% feasibility with mean reference gaps
of 1.63–3.26% on the primary constraints, 97.33–100% fea-
sibility when separately generated rules are composed for all
pairwise combinations, and 98.67% feasibility across twelve

--- PAGE 8 ---

additional constraint types. These results show that a shared
structured representation can connect natural-language inter-
pretation, deterministic action intervention, and inference-
time search guidance for a pretrained solver without retrain-
ing. Future work will study robustness to paraphrased and
noisier language, semantic rule verification, attribute-aware
critical-node extraction, stronger completion-aware decod-
ing, and broader optimization domains.

#### A.1


#### Base and Primary Constraint Datasets

A.1.1
Base CVRP benchmark
We use the public 300-
instance capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) test
split released with LLMCoSolver (Jiang et al. 2025a). Each
instance contains one depot and 10–100 customers. Using
the same numerical instances permits instance-level com-
parison between LLMCoSolver, which generates complete
routes from text, and LangNCO, which intervenes in a stan-
dard CVRP policy. The split also spans multiple sizes and
spatial distributions and includes a high-quality LKH-3 route
for every instance.
Throughout this supplement, V = {0, 1, . . . , n} is the
node set and the universe of decoder actions, with N =
|V | = n + 1 total nodes. Node 0 is the depot and nodes
1, . . . , n are the n customers. Customer i has raw coordinate
xi, raw demand di, and the homogeneous vehicle capacity is
Q. We write cij = ∥xi −xj∥2 for the unrounded Euclidean
edge cost between nodes i and j.
In the released generation procedure, the number of cus-
tomers is sampled from the integers in [10, 100]. Coordi-
nates are drawn either uniformly or from Gaussian mix-
ture distributions parameterized by the number of clusters
c and scale l, using (c, l) = (2, 5) and (3, 10). Customer
demands are sampled uniformly from 1, . . . , 10, while the
depot is assigned a demand value of zero as a represen-
tational convention inherited from the original dataset. In
the released implementation, if ¯d is the mean customer de-
mand and r ∼Triangular(3, 6, 25), the homogeneous vehi-
cle capacity is set to Q = ⌊r ¯d⌋. Capacity therefore depends
on each instance’s demand profile and sampled coefficient,
rather than being fixed by problem size.

A.1.2
Common construction protocol
For each task, a
single fixed sentence template is instantiated with instance-
specific nodes or parameters. Targets are selected from the
supplied reference-route structure, rather than independently
from coordinates and demands, so that the original route
violates the added requirement and a solver cannot succeed
merely by ignoring it. Where relevant, the construction also
excludes a cost-preserving route reversal as a trivial solution.
Every instance stores both the sentence shown to hu-
mans and LLMs and a machine-readable template with its
grounded variables. The latter is used only to build the post-
hoc checker. The instruction asks the solver to minimize
total distance, obey hard constraints, and satisfy soft re-
quests when possible. Wording is fixed within each task to
isolate constraint-type and composition performance from
paraphrase robustness. A single pseudorandom generator is
seeded once with 1234 at the beginning of each dataset-
construction run.
Labels above the braced positional slots state the value
inserted at run time and are not part of the recipe string. The

#### A.4


#### Reference Solvers and Evaluation

A.4.1
Independent post-hoc checker
Every method is
evaluated by the same checker, which receives only the re-
turned route sequence and is isolated from rule generation,
Rule Executor implementation, decoding, and rollout selec-
tion. A route is feasible only if it starts and ends at the depot,
respects capacity, visits every customer exactly once, and
satisfies every grounded additional constraint. Empty or un-
parseable outputs are infeasible. The objective is the sum
of unrounded Euclidean edge lengths over all routes, irre-
spective of any integer costs used internally by a reference
solver.
Road checks both directions of the closed edge. Time re-
constructs arrival times under the no-wait model. Vehicle
IDs are zero-based positions in the returned route list, and a
missing designated position is a violation. Order requires all
referenced nodes on one route and every prerequisite position
to precede the subject. Pairwise constraints are dispatched by
their task prefix and combined by conjunction. The additional
tasks use analogous task-specific checks derived from their
machine-readable records.
If DSL parsing, schema validation, and both repair at-
tempts all fail, LangNCO solves the instance without an
additional rule; the returned route is still checked against
the requested constraint. Runtime exceptions and decoding
deadlocks are recorded as no solution. No failure is removed
from the feasibility denominator.

#### B.1


#### DSL Schema, Validation, and Compilation

B.1.1
Typed
guarded-rule
representation
The
LangNCO DSL is a typed guarded mask-effect intermediate
representation. It separates four semantic components,
summarized in Table 5, rather than asking the LLM to
generate executable tensor code.
Targets may identify fixed nodes, structural sets such as
the depot or all customers, or sets selected from node at-
tributes. Expressions combine typed Boolean, comparison,
arithmetic, and visit-history aggregation primitives. Acces-
sors expose standard CVRP state, cumulative or vehicle-
specific visit history, node attributes, and explicitly declared
auxiliary observations. The latter may be scalar, node in-
dexed, vector valued, or pairwise. In these experiments,
elapsed time and travel times are auxiliary observations vis-
ible to the Rule Executor but not added to the frozen NCO
policy input.
For constraint c, the matched-action set is
Mc(st) = {a ∈Tc | gc(st) ∧hc(st, a)} ,
(19)
where Tc is the target set, gc(st) ∈{0, 1} is a state-level
guard, and hc(st, a) ∈{0, 1} is a candidate-level predicate.
With batch size B and N nodes, state guards are evaluated
in a row context and return B Booleans, whereas target pred-
icates and candidate guards are evaluated in a node context
and return B × N Booleans. Static context checks reject
candidate-indexed access where only a row value is valid.
This separation delegates broadcasting, tensor shapes, types,
and device placement to the compiler rather than the LLM.
B.1.2
Structured generation, validation, and compila-
tion
The generation protocol exposes a typed catalog of
available observations, target constructors, expression fami-
lies, and nonexpansive effects. It asks for one structured rule
representation without executable code or explanatory text.
For multiple requirements, accepted constraints are com-
bined in source order, keeping language interpretation sepa-
rate from deterministic execution.
After parsing and normalization, the rule is validated
against a machine-readable schema. The same compiler then
performs a dry compilation, rejecting unsupported opera-
tions, malformed target sets, missing arguments, and context-
invalid access. Dry compilation establishes structural con-
formance and static translatability, assuming run-time fields
have their declared shapes; it does not execute a route or
establish semantic agreement with the sentence.
On a parse, schema, or compile error, the prior output
and concrete diagnostic are returned to the LLM for at most
two bounded repairs. If all three attempts fail, the instance
is solved with only the base CVRP mask. This fallback pre-
serves pipeline execution but does not satisfy the omitted
requirement by default. The protocol describes the informa-
tion and guarantees supplied to the generator; exact prompt
wording and serialization details are not needed for the exe-
cution semantics analyzed here.

#### C.2


#### Randomness and Number of Runs

Each method is invoked once per test instance. Direct GPT-5
makes one request and does not retry API or parse failures.
DSL repair attempts are internal pipeline operations trig-
gered only by validation failure. LLMCoSolver’s eight can-
didates likewise form one best-of-eight invocation. The pro-
tocol does not estimate training-seed or API-generation vari-
ance. GPT-5 results are therefore not exactly reproducible.

#### C.3


#### Hardware, Software, and Timing Protocol

End-to-end latency is measured separately for each instance
and decomposed into (i) rule generation, including repair; (ii)
validation and DSL-to-Python compilation, or loading free-
form code; and (iii) NCO solving. Before timing Ollama,
one dummy request excludes initial model loading. The GPU
is synchronized immediately before and after NCO timing;
no separate NCO warm-up solve is used. Compilation in-
cludes validation, code generation, and function loading, but
excludes dataset and checkpoint loading, Ollama loading,
output writing, and post-hoc evaluation.
LangNCO processes the test instances sequentially, one
at a time with batch size one; its three measured stages are
also sequential within each instance. Direct GPT-5 requests
were issued in parallel, so their total wall time is not directly
comparable. Observed API latency includes network trans-
fer, service queueing, and service-side inference; Ollama la-
tency also includes local client–server communication. The
reported software versions, timing boundaries, and execu-
tion order define the protocol used for the end-to-end latency
measurements in Table 13.

D.2.3
Critical-Offset sensitivity
We vary two parame-
ters while holding the total budget at B = 100 rollouts
per POMO start and augmentation. The critical-offset roll-
out ratio ρ assigns Boff = ρB rollouts to nonzero offsets
and leaves the rest at α = 0. The offset-strength spac-
ing ∆α defines Λ∆α = {∆α, 2∆α, . . . , 1.0}; its values
are assigned cyclically to the offset rollouts. We test ρ ∈
{0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50} and ∆α ∈{0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50},
excluding three combinations with Boff < |Λ∆α|. As in
the decoding ablation, reference masks isolate decoder be-
havior, while critical nodes are extracted from LangNCO-
Gemma4 DSL. The setting used in the main paper is
(ρ, ∆α) = (0.10, 0.20).
All 13 evaluated settings reach 100% feasibility on all
four datasets. In particular, ρ = 0.05 uses only five offset-
guided rollouts, yet both evaluable spacings recover a feasible
solution for every instance. The feasibility gains therefore do
not depend on a narrow allocation or strength grid: mixing a
small number of directed trajectories into ordinary sampling
is already sufficient to avoid the observed future deadlocks.
Unlike feasibility, conditional solution quality does vary
across the parameter grid. At ∆α = 0.20, increasing ρ from
0.05 to 0.50 lowers the Vehicle gap from 2.60% to 2.33%
and the Order gap from 2.89% to 2.72%; Time improves
more modestly from 1.94% to 1.85%. This indicates that
allocating more of the fixed budget to guided trajectories can
improve the best feasible route found, even after feasibility
has saturated at 100%.
The effect of ∆α is task-dependent rather than uniformly
smaller. On Order, changing ∆α at fixed ρ produces up to a
0.18-point gap range, comparable to the effect of changing
ρ at fixed ∆α. At ρ = 0.50, the two finer grids give 2.59–
2.60%, versus 2.74% for ∆α = 0.50. Thus, ρ controls how
many guided candidates are available, whereas ∆α controls
the coverage and resolution of intervention strengths. Both
can matter for finding a route that remains aligned with the
base policy while satisfying the constraint.
The task pattern suggests a mechanism for this quality
effect. Under ordinary hard-masked sampling, Vehicle and
Order exhibit both more deadlocks and higher conditional
gaps than Road and Time (Table 14). Hard masking guaran-
tees only immediate legality: in Vehicle, other customers can
consume capacity needed by assigned nodes, while in Order,
a depot return can close a route before all order-linked cus-
tomers are completed. Once such a future consequence be-
comes immediate, the mask can abruptly remove the policy’s
preferred actions. If legal actions remain, renormalization
forces choices that the learned policy originally considered
unlikely, disrupting its distance-minimizing route structure
through a costly detour; otherwise, the rollout deadlocks. A
larger ρ creates more trajectories that prioritize critical nodes
before this point, while a finer ∆α grid includes weak off-
sets that preserve the base policy as well as strong offsets
that avert deadlock. Selecting among these trajectories can
therefore recover a policy-aligned route rather than merely
a feasible one. Time also creates a future dependency, but
each instance has a single windowed target rather than mul-
tiple coupled critical nodes, which may explain its smaller

### E


### Failure and Mechanistic Analyses


#### E.1


#### Free-Form Python Failures

Free-form failures concentrate in low-level tensor integration
rather than in recognizing the requested condition. Gemma4
produces 11 Time and 32 Order runtime failures; GPT-5
produces one Vehicle implementation failure. Three repre-
sentative mechanisms follow.
Shape mismatch.
A Time rule adds curr_time of shape
[B] directly to travel_times of shape [B, N], causing
a broadcasting error. The rule needs only the designated
destination and should jointly index the batch, current node,
and target to obtain a [B] pairwise value.
Device mismatch.
An Order rule initializes
met = torch.ones(can_visit.shape[0], dtype
=torch.bool)
on the CPU, while visited_by, vehicle_idx,
and can_visit reside on the GPU. A subsequent
Boolean operation fails unless the new tensor is created on
can_visit.device.
Copy-only advanced indexing.
A Vehicle rule uses
can_visit[rows][:, cols] = False
The first Boolean index creates a temporary copy, so the
assignment does not modify can_visit. This silent fail-
ure requires a single direct index into the original tensor.
The DSL avoids all three classes by leaving shapes, devices,
broadcasting, and in-place mask composition to the compiler.

#### E.2


#### Pairwise and Additional-Constraint Failures

Separate Vehicle + Order generation can produce indi-
vidually plausible rules whose conjunction loses a cross-
constraint implication. For example:
- Nodes 30, 3 must be served by Vehicle
ID 5.
- Nodes 30, 60 must be served before Node
57 by the same vehicle.
The Vehicle rule restricts only nodes 30 and 3. The Order
rule blocks 57 until 30 and 60 have been visited by the current
vehicle, but independent generation does not infer that the
shared node 30 makes vehicle 5 the required vehicle for 60
and 57 as well. If another vehicle serves 60 first, vehicle 5
cannot meet the route-local prerequisite. Separate generation
fails on two such instances; Joint constrains all four related
nodes to vehicle 5 and completes both.
Joint generation is not uniformly better. One Road + Time
rule checks whether current_time itself lies in the win-
dow rather than adding travel time to the target. Several Road
+ Order and Time + Order rules omit by: vehicle_idx
from all_visited; this represents global precedence but
loses the requested same-vehicle condition. The frequency
of the latter error in Road + Order may indicate interference
from the road’s node-to-node relation, but that interpretation
is an inference from outputs, not a causal finding. Joint Vehi-
cle + Order also sometimes misses the same cross-constraint
implication. Moreover, an OR-Tools no-solution result under
the time limit is not proof of infeasibility, so we assign mech-
anistic causes only when the generated rule or trace directly
identifies one.
Small-delivery assignment exposes a different limitation.
The Rule Executor correctly evaluates a where target such as
“demand at most 4” at every step, but the static critical-node
extractor resolves only literal IDs. The designated route can
return before serving every member of the attribute-defined
set, after which other vehicles are blocked from them.
For diagnosis, we preserve the same hard constraint and

Vehicle ID 0 must serve all and only
customer nodes whose demand is at most 4.

5, 8, 10, 12, 18, 19, 27, 29, 41, 42, 46,

#### Critical-Offset Sampling Case Study

We compare an Ordinary Sampling rollout that deadlocks

E.3.1
Time: preserving a service opportunity
Both
rollouts first move from the depot to node 14. Under Ordi-
nary Sampling, node 2 remains legal but has raw probability
1.34 × 10−9; selecting node 13 makes its time window un-
reachable and the rollout deadlocks. In the Critical-Offset
rollout, node 2 has raw probability 5.69 × 10−9 but offset
weight 1.00, so it is served immediately and the rollout com-
pletes.
The hard mask cannot prohibit node 13 because it is lo-
cally legal. The offset adds a trajectory that uses the expiring
service opportunity.
In this representative case, a critical node is legal but re-
ceives negligible policy probability. The Rule Executor has
no basis to remove a different action that is legal at the current
step. Critical-Offset Sampling reallocates part of the existing
rollout budget toward constraint-referenced nodes without
relaxing masks. The lower aggregate gaps for Critical-Offset
Sampling in Table 14 are consistent with this interpretation,
but the case study does not establish it causally. The method
remains a search bias, not a look-ahead proof of completion,
and its current fixed-ID coverage is the limitation diagnosed
above.
