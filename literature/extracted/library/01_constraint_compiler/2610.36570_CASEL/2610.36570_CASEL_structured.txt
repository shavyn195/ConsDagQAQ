Abstract—Tool-using language agents increasingly modify
external systems, yet most runtime safeguards allocate scrutiny by
generic uncertainty scores or coarse mutation heuristics. We intro-
duce CASEL (Consequence-Aware Selective Execution Layer), a
model-agnostic runtime allocation policy that combines calibrated
failure probability, state-dependent consequence, reversibility,
ambiguity, and evidence freshness to choose, for each proposed tool
call, among direct execution, verification, sandboxing, clarification,
approval, and abstention by minimizing a per-mode cost. We
formalize direct execution as selective prediction and state and
test the validity conditions of a finite-sample threshold and a
trajectory risk ledger. In a controlled simulation over 20 seeds
with 120,000 test actions each, in which every method has the
same intervention menu and the same compute cost, CASEL
reduces harmful-side-effect trajectories from 14.75 percent for
an uncertainty-ranked gate to 7.02 percent and mean normalized
harm from 0.184 to 0.123. A gate ranked by the product of failure
probability and consequence reaches similar harm (5.82 percent,
0.120) but sends 45 percent more actions to human approval,
and removing reversibility-aware routing raises the rate to 15.53
percent. A trajectory-level risk budget, in contrast, increases
harm at equal cost (11.82 percent). Intervention effectiveness is
assumed, not measured: the results rank allocation rules under
the simulator’s assumptions and do not establish deployment
effectiveness.
Index Terms—LLM agents, tool use, reliability, selective
prediction, uncertainty calibration, runtime verification, risk-
aware execution.
I. INTRODUCTION
Language-model agents increasingly act on external systems
over long trajectories, and as models grow more capable they

##### become autonomous domain-specific agents, e.g., SP-Mind


##### runs complete spatial-proteomics analyses with specialized


##### tools.[1] They now also generate device configurations for


##### multi-vendor optical transport networks,[2] where an erro-


##### neous change can affect live services. Benchmarks such


##### as WebArena, τ-Bench/τ 2-Bench, and AgentDojo expose


##### failures from stale state, planning errors, unsafe tool use,

and adversarial observations.[3]–[6] Reflection and self-critique
improve reasoning,[7], [8] and recent work studies clarification,

##### abstention, mutating-action verification, least privilege, and


##### transactional containment.[9]–[13] These mechanisms address

different failure sources but leave a common allocation question:
how much scrutiny should be spent before a particular action
is allowed to change the world?
Uncertainty alone does not answer it. Two actions may share
a 20% probability of error, yet a read-only search is cheap to
retry while an irreversible transfer is costly; an uncertainty-only
gate treats them alike, and a mutation-only gate still conflates
a reversible edit with an irreversible commitment.
We propose CASEL, a runtime layer between an arbitrary

##### base agent and its tool executor (Figure 1). At each step


##### the controller estimates failure probability ˆpt, consequence


##### ˆct, reversibility ρt, ambiguity gt, and evidence staleness dt,

and selects a graded intervention. Scrutiny should scale with
consequence-weighted risk rather than generic confidence, the
intervention should match the action’s reversibility, and direct

##### execution should be calibrated as selective prediction.[14]–[16]

CASEL is therefore not another uncertainty gate but a runtime

##### allocation policy that decides where scrutiny is worth its


##### cost and calls existing safety mechanisms as its intervention

branches.

##### Our contributions are: (i) a consequence-aware execution


##### objective with six intervention modes, five of which are

VI. CONTROLLED EVALUATION
A. Protocol
Each seed has an independent 40,000-action calibration split
and 6,000 test trajectories of 20 actions (120,000 test actions).
Actions are read-only (58%), reversible (28%), or irreversible

##### (14%). True failure probabilities come from class-dependent

Beta distributions with a trajectory-level difficulty perturbation,
creating within-task dependence; consequences lie mainly in
[0.02, 0.20], [0.15, 0.70], and [0.55, 1] for the three classes. The
controller sees a noisy raw failure score, isotonic-calibrated on
the calibration split, and a noisy consequence estimate.

##### The simulator instantiates direct, verify, sandbox, and


##### approve with residual multipliers γm of 1.00, 0.42, 0.15,


##### and 0.04, compute costs k(m) of 0, 1.0, 1.6, and 2.2, and


##### interaction costs q(m) of 0, 0, 0.04, and 0.65. These are


##### assumed constants. Direct and verify are always feasible,


##### sandbox only for reversible mutations, and approve only for


##### irreversible ones with ˆct ≥0.55. Clarify is exercised only


##### in the ambiguity stress family (Sec. VI-E); abstain and the

staleness branch (dt, τd) are not exercised. The cost weights
keep λ : µ = 0.02 : 0.015; a common factor sets the operating
point.

##### Comparators. Verify-only gates (the usual scalar gates)


##### verify the actions ranked highest by ˆp or ˆc. Menu gates


##### rank by ˆp, ˆc, or ˆpˆc and give the top-ranked actions their

strongest feasible intervention (sandbox if reversible, approve
if irreversible, otherwise verify), so they have CASEL’s menu.
CASEL minimizes J(m). Ablations: CASEL-NoRev (verify
only), CASEL+Budget (λ = 0.02 with the ledger, BH = 0.08,

##### ¯rt = ˆptˆct), and CASEL-Uncal (raw score). All of these are

matched to one compute cost, 0.486 (for CASEL, cost-weight
factor 0.58 ± 0.02), using scores but no outcome labels; Base,
Always-Verify, and Mutation-Gated are fixed policies.
Metrics. An action routed to m fails with probability γmpt,

##### using one uniform draw per action shared by all methods

(paired outcomes). Task success is the fraction of trajectories
with no failed action; the harmful side-effect rate (HSR) is the

##### fraction with at least one failed action of true consequence


##### ct ≥0.45; mean normalized harm (MNH) is the mean over


##### trajectories of �20


##### t=1 ctFt. Direct coverage is the share of

directly executed actions, cost the mean k(m) per action, and
approvals the share of actions sent to human approval. Results
average 20 seeds.

VII. DEPLOYMENT CONSIDERATIONS

##### Adaptation and drift. Only the signals in cbase, Mscope


##### and the preferred high-risk modes are domain-specific; in

optical networks, for instance, alarm correlation and root-alarm
detection[28] show how far a fault propagates and can inform
the scope factor. λ, µ are read off the deployment’s own cost–

##### harm frontier (Figure 4), and γm is estimated conservatively

on trajectory-disjoint traces. Such gates must be recalibrated
per domain: RAGScope’s gate transfers poorly across sources

##### but recovers with 100–200 target labels.[21] When logged


##### calibration or residual-risk drift leaves its tolerance region,
