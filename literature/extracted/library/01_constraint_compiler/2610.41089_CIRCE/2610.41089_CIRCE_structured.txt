## CIRCE Divines LLM Task Complexity from Constraint Structure


### Chenhao Hong


### Jiakai Xu


### Kaushiki Singh


### Yusen Zhang


### Eugene Wu


### Columbia University


### {chenhao.hong,ax2155,ks4402,yz5296}@columbia.edu


### ewu@cs.columbia.edu


### Abstract

Estimating whether a problem is too complex
for a large language model (LLM) before the
model attempts it can guide model routing, how
to allocate test-time compute, and suggest the
need for new tools to aid the agent. While
existing estimators rely on the problem text,
LLM-annotated task difficulty labels, or sig-
nals obtained after a model attempts to solve
the problem, such as its hidden states, none
explicitly leverage the constraint structure in-
herent in the problem: the variables to assign
and how the constraints over them interact.
We introduce CIRCE,1 which extracts a prob-
lem’s structure by using a cheap LLM to con-
struct a Constraint Intermediate Representation
that deterministically compiles into the con-
straint graph. Across seven reasoning bench-
marks (including SAT, logic, and math) and
four LLMs, even a randomly initialized GNN
encoder improves predictions of LLM response
correctness, reducing negative log-likelihood
from 0.465 to 0.423 and increasing AUROC
from 0.691 to 0.727 relative to the strongest
baseline for each metric.

### 1


### Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly
asked to solve complex problems in which con-
straints create dependencies among decisions.
These problems include constraint satisfaction (e.g.,
arranging wedding seating while keeping some
guests together and others apart), resource man-
agement and scheduling (e.g., assigning staff to
shifts while respecting availability and coverage
requirements), logical entailment (e.g., determin-
ing whether an expense qualifies for reimbursement
under a company’s policy), and database editing un-
der integrity constraints (e.g., deleting a customer
record without leaving orders linked to a nonex-
istent customer). Although task success may be

assessed from an LLM’s result, it remains difficult
to predict in advance whether a particular problem

Complexity estimation predicts the probability
that a given LLM will answer a problem correctly
under a fixed inference protocol (prompting and
generation settings). Estimates made before gener-
ation can support model routing (Chen et al., 2023;
Ong et al., 2025), allocation of test-time compute
(Snell et al., 2024), and abstention on likely failures
(Kamath et al., 2020).
Existing approaches use text embeddings of the
problem (Pacchiardi et al., 2025), LLM-generated
ratings of required abilities such as logical reason-
ing and domain knowledge (Zhou et al., 2026), or
the target model’s internal activations (Zhu et al.,
2025). The latter requires internal model access
unavailable in proprietary model APIs. These ap-
proaches also do not explicitly represent the prob-
lem’s variables and interacting constraints.
Constraint structure helps predict computational
difficulty: random 3-SAT formulas are hardest near
a critical clause-to-variable ratio (Mitchell et al.,
1992), and SATzilla uses structural features to pre-
dict runtimes and select the solver expected to
be fastest (Xu et al., 2008). Constraint structure
also guides LLM task decomposition (Zhou et al.,
2025).
We therefore ask: (1) does constraint structure
predict LLM response correctness, and (2) can it
be recovered reliably from problem text?
One challenge is that natural-language prob-
lems often do not specify variables and constraints
explicitly, and LLM-generated constraint graphs
are unreliable: only 136 of 200 Hamiltonian path
graphs are correct in our evaluation (68.0%; Ta-
ble 3). Our key observation is that an LLM need
only describe the variables, constraints, and query
in an intermediate representation, which a deter-
ministic compiler converts into a graph.
We introduce Constraint Intermediate Represen-

tation for Complexity Estimation (CIRCE), a work-
flow built on this separation of semantic extraction
and graph construction. On the Hamiltonian path,
this approach raises correctness to 92.5%. We de-
velop 41 handcrafted graph features (CIRCE-Stats)
and find that richer feature sets generally improve
prediction. To capture interactions beyond aggre-
gate statistics, we also use a graph neural network
(CIRCE-GNN). We compare frozen encoders with
random initialization or self-supervised pretrain-
ing on separate unlabeled problems. A lightweight,
model-specific predictor maps either representation
to the probability of a correct response.
We evaluate CIRCE against four prediction base-
lines and a constant predictor on seven reason-
ing benchmarks and four LLMs. Five-fold cross-
validation over 200 problems per benchmark fits
each predictor on approximately 160 training prob-
lems, with ten responses per problem supplying
correctness labels. Relative to the strongest base-
line overall for each metric, the pretrained variant
reduces average negative log-likelihood of response
correctness by 9.5% (0.465 to 0.421) and increases
AUROC by 3.9 percentage points (0.691 to 0.730).
Even a frozen, randomly initialized GNN encoder
outperforms every baseline on average and nearly
matches the pretrained variant. These results show
that constraint structure recovered from text is a
useful signal for predicting LLM failures before
generation. Text-to-SQL and program synthesis
are promising targets for future evaluation.

### 2


### Related Work

2.1
Complexity Estimation for LLMs
Recent work predicts instance-level LLM perfor-
mance using textual assessors (Pacchiardi et al.,
2025; Schellaert et al., 2025), semantic task-
demand annotations (Zhou et al., 2026), or the
target model’s hidden representations (Zhu et al.,
2025).
Complementary work estimates diffi-
culty from problem content or answer plausibil-
ity (Truong et al., 2025; Mozafari et al., 2026), or
uses difficulty estimates to adapt reasoning strate-
gies (Xu et al., 2024). A related line of work es-
timates response correctness using elicited confi-
dence, inter-model response agreement, or histor-
ical correctness patterns (Tian et al., 2023; Xia
et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026). Although these ap-
proaches provide useful textual, semantic, represen-
tational, and behavioral signals, they do not explic-
itly model reasoning problems as constraint struc-
tures with interacting variables and constraints.
2.2
Structured Representations for LLM
Reasoning
Prior work has explored symbolic representations
for LLM reasoning (Pan et al., 2023; Olausson
et al., 2023), relationships between problem struc-
ture and reasoning complexity (Kang et al., 2024),
and limitations in LLM reasoning over complex
data structures (He et al., 2026). Related work ex-
amines formal specification inference and reliable
theorem proving (Le-Cong et al., 2025; Quan et al.,
2025). Other studies examine compositional prob-
lem complexity (Xie et al., 2026) and constraint-
based problem decomposition (Zhou et al., 2025).
Research on multi-constraint instruction following
further shows that constraint composition and de-
pendencies affect model performance (Wen et al.,
2024; Liu et al., 2026). While these studies show
that problem structure and constraints matter for
LLM reasoning, whether structural features ex-
tracted from a common constraint representation
can predict correctness on individual problem in-
stances remains underexplored.
2.3
Learning from Graph Representations
Studies show that LLMs struggle with structured
graph reasoning problems, motivating explicit rep-
resentations of relationships and dependencies (Dai
et al., 2025; Agrawal et al., 2024). Structural prop-
erties have also been used to predict solver per-
formance through handcrafted features (Xu et al.,
2008) and graph-based learning (Chang et al., 2022;
Fu et al., 2025). Graph neural networks, in contrast,
learn representations through neighborhood aggre-
gation (Kipf and Welling, 2017; Xu et al., 2019),
including for language reasoning over knowledge
graphs (Yasunaga et al., 2021). Self-supervised
methods such as GraphMAE (Hou et al., 2022)
further enable graph representation learning with-
out problem-specific labels. However, rather than
using these representations to solve problems or
support reasoning directly, we leverage the con-
straint structure to estimate the empirical difficulty
of individual problems for a target LLM.

### 3


### Method

The way constraints connect variables can affect
the reasoning required to answer a problem. We use
these relationships as a signal for complexity esti-
mation. Extracting them directly from the problem
using an LLM is challenging. CIRCE addresses

this by asking the LLM to describe the variables
and constraints in an intermediate representation
(CIR), from which a rule-based compiler constructs
the graph.
Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of CIRCE. We
first extract a CIR and compile it into a constraint
graph (Section 3.2). We then represent the graph
using handcrafted features in CIRCE-Stats (Sec-
tion 3.3) or a GNN with or without self-supervised
pretraining in CIRCE-GNN (Section 3.4). Finally,
a gradient-boosted predictor maps the structural
representation to a correctness probability for the
target model (Section 3.5).

3.2
From Problem to Constraint Graph
A CIR describes variables, constraints, types, do-
mains, and the requested query. We prompt an
LLM to translate the problem into this represen-
tation without solving it or asserting an answer.
A rule-based graph builder checks the CIR for-
mat, reports parsing errors for correction, and com-
piles accepted outputs into a typed constraint graph
G = (V, E).
There are two types of graph nodes. Factor
nodes represent constraints and the query, while
variable nodes represent declared variables, propo-
sitions, entities, and shared domain values. Edges
connect factor nodes to their corresponding vari-
able nodes. For example, 50B=W creates a factor
connected to bag count B and weight W; C=18B
creates another connected to cost C and B. The
query factor connects to the requested cost C.
The same graph builder is shared across bench-
marks. Both syntax checking and semantic vali-
dation help assess whether the CIR preserves the
problem’s variables, constraints, and query. Au-
tomated checks can trigger repair. Appendix B
provides the checks, repairs, and validation details.
3.3
CIRCE-Stats: Handcrafted Features
Structural features have been used to predict the
difficulty of constraint-solving problems, as exem-
plified by SATzilla (Xu et al., 2008). As a first step
of examining whether such features can be used
to predict the correctness of LLMs on constraint
problems, we introduce CIRCE-Stats, where we
handcrafted 41 features to capture the structural
information of the constraint graph: size (5), factor
occurrences (4), degree (8), topology (5), query
(3), and CIR operators (16). Appendix C lists the
features and their definitions.

4.2
Models and Benchmarks
Our methods and baselines are evaluated on
Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026a), Qwen3.8-27B
(Qwen Team, 2026b), Inkling (Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026) and GPT-6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026), rep-
resenting the latest generation of open-weight and
proprietary LLMs at the time of our study.
A diverse set of reasoning benchmarks with ex-
plicit or implicit constraints is used (Table 1). For
each benchmark, a subset of the problems is used
for evaluation, and a larger, non-overlapping subset
is used for pretraining our GNN. Qwen3.8-27B is
used to generate the CIR for all the benchmarks
(Appendix B). We set the thinking effort of the
models based on the difficulty of each benchmark
to prevent saturated accuracy on easier benchmarks
(Appendix A). Among the models we evaluate,
Qwen3.5-9B is the only model that we can deploy
locally given the limitations of model weight ac-
cess and GPU memory. It is therefore used as the
only model to evaluate the Already Knows predic-
tor. To match the behavior of the models used in
the original paper, we set the thinking mode to off
for Qwen3.5-9B.

5.2
Qualitative Analysis
Graph representations versus statistics.
The
Logical benchmark shows the largest average ad-
vantage of CIRCE-GNN over CIRCE-Stats. To
examine what the graph representation adds, con-
sider two problems about the same seven golfers,
L1 and L2 (Figure 3). Their six ordering clues
are identical: Amy finishes last, Ana third-to-last,
Eve third, and Eli above Dan above Ada above
Mel. L1 asks who finishes last, and L2 asks who
finishes second-to-last. Forty of their 41 Stats fea-
tures match exactly, including their graph sizes and
operator counts.
The relevant difference is how the query con-
nects to the stated constraints. In L1, the query’s
position node is shared with the clue fixing Amy’s
position. In L2, the requested position has no such
fixed-position clue. Identifying its occupant re-
quires combining the ordering chain with the oc-
cupied positions. Message passing can distinguish
these query neighborhoods, whereas the present
aggregate statistics largely omit their arrangement.
For Qwen3.5-9B, CIRCE-GNN-P predicts 0.804
and 0.282, consistent with the observed success
rates of 10/10 and 0/10. CIRCE-Stats predicts
0.366 and 0.503, reversing their ordering. This
pair illustrates a structural distinction the GNN can
represent.

Explicit constraints versus external assessors.
A GSM8K problem asks the cost of 850 kilograms
of coal, delivered in 50-kilogram bags costing
18 dollars each. The CIR exposes the relations
W = 850, 50B = W and C = 18B, where W
is the total weight, B the number of bags and C
the requested cost. Its graph is a short, acyclic de-
pendency chain: each quantity depends on one pre-
ceding quantity, without branching alternatives or
additional interacting conditions. These properties
provide a concrete description of the required rea-
soning independent of the story’s wording. CIRCE-
Stats summarizes such graph properties, while
CIRCE-GNN learns from the connected sequence
of constraint types.
In comparison, ADeLe represents the prob-
lem through broad reasoning-demand scores, Pre-
dictaBoard uses a text embedding, and Already
Knows uses the target model’s initial hidden state.
These inputs do not explicitly supply the extracted

5.3
Reliability of CIR
We measure two metrics to assess the reliability of
the CIRs: parsability and correctness. Parsability
measures whether the CIR has a valid syntax, and
correctness measures whether the CIR correctly
represents the constraint structure of the problem.
For SATBench, Logical, and Hamiltonian, a rule-
based checker can verify the constraint structure,
so we check both parsability and correctness on
all 200 problems. For FOLIO, NLCO, Zebra, and
GSM8K, rule-based checker only covers part of
the constraint structure, so we check parsability on
all 200 problems and manually review the correct-
ness of 20 randomly sampled source–CIR pairs per
benchmark. On the three benchmarks with a full
checker, we also check the parsability and correct-
ness of direct graph generation, where the LLM is
prompted to generate the graph directly from the
natural-language question.

### 6


### Conclusion

CIRCE demonstrates that constraint structure re-
covered from problem text is a useful signal for
predicting LLM task complexity before generation.
Strong results from handcrafted statistics and ran-
domly initialized GNNs show that this signal can be
used without encoder pretraining. Graph encodings
improve over statistics on average, with pretrain-
ing providing further small gains. These findings
support separating task representation from model-
specific prediction: a shared constraint graph can
serve different target models through lightweight
predictors trained on black-box labels. Extending
this representation to richer constraints and queries
is a promising direction for future work.

### 7


### Limitations

While CIRCE applies to a wide range of reasoning
benchmarks, its scope is limited to the problems
whose constraints are part of the input. It does
not cover the problems where inferring constraints
is part of the reasoning process. CIRCE also es-
timates only the part of problem complexity that
constraint structure reflects, while other methods
that depend on the model’s states could potentially
reveal the failure cases such as a misread question.
Combining our method with their features remains
future work.
We showed the reliability of the CIRs, but they
are generated by a single LLM, and their correct-
ness cannot be fully verified: rule-based checkers
cover three benchmarks, and the other four were
spot-checked on 20 questions each. We also used
a fixed set of problems for pretraining the GNN
encoder and evaluating the predictors. Evaluating
how the number of problems affects the encoder
and predictor performance would further establish
the scalability of our method. Exploring predictor
transfer across benchmarks and models could pro-
vide further evidence of the generalizability of our
method.

C.1
Encoder and Pretraining
Kind, subtype and factor-type embeddings have
dimensions 8, 8 and 4, with 5, 64 and 4 categories.
Node subtypes record variable or entity types and
predicate argument counts. The 20 embedding co-
ordinates and 16 log-transformed operator counts
are projected from 36 to 64 dimensions with ReLU
and layer normalization.
Each of the six layers has role/polarity embed-
dings of size 32 × 64/4 × 64, a learned scalar ϵ, a
64–128–64 MLP, ReLU, dropout 0.1, and residual
layer normalization. Readout concatenates sum,
mean, query sum, and population SD into 256 di-
mensions, using variance stabilizer 10−8.
Node/edge masking probabilities are 0.4/0.2.
Learned vectors replace masked attributes; reverse
edges share a mask and connectivity is retained.
One mask is forced when a nonempty set receives
none. Six equally weighted losses reconstruct kind,
subtype, factor type, operator counts, edge role,
and polarity. Subtype applies to non-factors, fac-

--- PAGE 15 ---

tor type/operators to factors, and edge losses to
one orientation per masked pair. Empty losses are
zero. Linear decoders use 64D node states or 128D
endpoint concatenations.
We use AdamW, learning rate 0.001, weight de-
cay 0.001, batch size 32, gradient-norm clipping 5,
and fixed 100 epochs. Each epoch samples 4,684
graphs with equal source weight; validation bal-
ances 516 entries with mask seed 777001. Encoder
seeds are 20260919, 20260922, 20260923, and
20260924. Evaluation freezes the encoder and dis-

Last-layer
4,096D states train a 4096–64–1 MLP with GELU
