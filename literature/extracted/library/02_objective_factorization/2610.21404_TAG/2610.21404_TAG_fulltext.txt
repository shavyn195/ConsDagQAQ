ABSTRACT
To improve task performance while encouraging desirable response properties,
LLM post-training often combines a primary reward (e.g., correctness) with aux-
iliary rewards (e.g., brevity or efficiency). However, how these rewards are aggre-
gated can critically affect training outcomes. We show that symmetric aggrega-
tion, which treats reward channels equally, can induce a failure where aggregate
reward improves while primary-task performance degrades, even when individual
rewards are meaningful. We investigate this aggregation-induced failure through
a one-step multi-reward decision formulation, characterizing when it constitutes
multi-reward hacking. We prove that, under symmetric aggregation, deterministic
conditional-mean GRPO converges to auxiliary-favoring behavior when auxiliary
rewards are more reliably obtained, regardless of initialization. We propose Trust-
Anchored Gating (TAG), which first elects an anchor channel based on the policy
drift induced by optimizing each channel alone, then gates auxiliary credit on an-
chor satisfaction. Unlike calibration-sensitive static reweighting, TAG structurally
eliminates credit for auxiliary-only outcomes while preserving additional credit
for joint success. In the same analytical setting, TAG provably reverses this se-
lection, converging to the primary-favoring action from any initialization when
the primary channel is anchored. Experiments across Qwen and Llama mod-
els on mathematics and program synthesis reveal substantial degradation under
symmetric, constrained, and normalized aggregation, whereas TAG mitigates this
failure. On Qwen3-4B with a brevity reward, TAG achieves 0.581 pass@4 ver-
sus 0.220 for additive aggregation, while shortening responses by 44% relative to
correctness-only training.

focus on how to combine reward signals, leaving open when and why aggregation systematically
favors auxiliary behaviors over the primary objective. This paper asks the question that this practice
leaves open: Can reward aggregation itself undermine a primary objective, even when that objective
is explicitly rewarded?
It can, and the failure has a characteristic shape (Figure 1). Across models, tasks and aggregation
rules the auxiliary rate climbs toward one, so the aggregated objective keeps rising, while task cor-
rectness collapses (Figures 1 and 5). In most of these runs the policy stops solving the task and locks
onto a single behavior that games the auxiliary signal (Tables 1–2; Appendix C.5): it negates its final
answer to pass a sign check, shrinks its solution to a bare few-token guess to earn a brevity reward,
and emits programs that exit at once and solve nothing to earn a latency reward. Length exploitation
of this kind is documented in RLHF (Singhal et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), and reasoning models
learn to exploit unit tests in agentic coding (Baker et al., 2025). We call this failure multi-reward
hacking (Definition 2), an instance of reward hacking (Amodei et al., 2016; Skalse et al., 2022b) with
a distinctive origin: unlike failures of misspecified or incomplete proxies (Pan et al., 2022; Zhuang
& Hadfield-Menell, 2020), it occurs with legitimate channels as well as flawed ones; aggregation
itself induces it. Existing reward hacking studies largely focus on imperfect proxies (Gao et al.,
2023; Karwowski et al., 2024), while detection methods typically assess individual reward signals
or behaviors. Neither fully explains how combining rewards can systematically favor an auxiliary
objective over a primary one. Recent multi-reward methods that rebalance the channels within each
group, such as MO-GRPO (Ichihara et al., 2026) and PG-OT (Ba et al., 2026), remain symmetric in
the channels and, as we show, collapse in the same way (Section 6).
We first establish a fundamental failure mode of symmetric reward aggregation, where no reward
channel is given priority over another. Such rules assign equal credit to primary-only and auxiliary-
only success, despite differences in how easily these rewards can be obtained. We formalize this
in a stochastic decision model that captures a common feature of real-world post-training: primary
rewards are often harder to obtain than auxiliary rewards. For example, solving a math problem or
passing unit tests requires successful reasoning, whereas producing a short response or a program
that exits immediately is comparatively easy. We prove that, under this imbalance, every symmetric
rule that rewards single-channel success drives the conditional-mean GRPO dynamics toward the
auxiliary-favoring behavior, regardless of initialization, even when the policy initially favors the
primary objective. This result reveals that symmetric aggregation can systematically favor an easier
auxiliary objective at the expense of the primary one.
The failure motivates breaking the symmetry in reward aggregation. While the analysis assumes a
designated primary channel, identifying which reward to prioritize in practice is not always straight-
forward. This raises two questions: which channel should receive priority, and how should that
priority be enforced? We propose Trust-Anchored Gating (TAG), which first elects an anchor by
briefly optimizing each channel in isolation and selecting the one that induces the least policy drift.
The intuition is that exploitable channels may induce large policy shifts toward shortcut behaviors,
making isolated drift a useful signal for anchor selection. Our experiments show that lowering the
auxiliary weight delays the collapse without preventing it, and that a weight which is safe at one
model scale fails at another (§6.2). Rather than relying on static reweighting, TAG enforces priority
through a structural gate: auxiliary-only success earns no more credit than failure on both channels,
while joint success remains more rewarding than primary-only success. We prove that, with the
primary channel as the anchor, TAG drives the conditional-mean GRPO dynamics to the primary-
favoring action from any initialization, for any positive gating weight, whenever primary success
remains possible.
We validate these findings on Qwen and Llama models (1.7B–30B), on mathematics and program
synthesis, with compatible and incompatible auxiliary rewards. In nearly every setting, previous
aggregation trades task correctness for the auxiliary reward, whereas TAG substantially mitigates
this degradation while retaining auxiliary benefits. On Qwen3-4B, for instance, TAG reaches 0.581
pass@4 under a brevity reward (0.220 for additive aggregation) with responses 44% shorter than
correctness-only training. Our contributions are:
• A provable failure of symmetric aggregation. We formalize multi-reward hacking and prove that,
when auxiliary rewards are more reliably obtained, symmetric aggregation drives the conditional-
mean GRPO dynamics toward auxiliary-favoring behavior from any initialization. We further char-
acterize when this selection constitutes reward hacking under the unknown true reward.

RELATED WORK
Reward hacking and over-optimization in LLM post-training. Optimizing a proxy reward be-
yond the region where it tracks the intended objective is a well-studied failure mode (Amodei et al.,
2016; Pan et al., 2022; Zhuang & Hadfield-Menell, 2020). Skalse et al. (2022b) formalize hackabil-
ity as a reversal of the policy ordering induced by proxy and true reward, and Laidlaw et al. (2025)
refine the notion to correlated proxies. Over-optimization has been measured along the KL path
(Gao et al., 2023) and in occupancy-measure geometry (Karwowski et al., 2024), and addressed
by regularizing the occupancy measure toward the reference policy’s (Laidlaw et al., 2025) or by
aligning the policy update direction (Deng et al., 2026); action-level KL regularization alone fails
under heavy-tailed misspecification (Kwa et al., 2024). Length exploitation is the best-documented
instance in RLHF (Singhal et al., 2024), and grader loopholes let a wrong answer pass the verifier
(Wang et al., 2026); ODIN (Chen et al., 2024) separates length and quality heads and optimizes only
the latter. These lines concern one proxy against the true reward; here the optimized scalar is itself
a combination of channels, and the failure arises from their interaction.
Multi-reward and rubric-based RL for LLMs. Practice combines channels by weighted sums
with fixed or learned weights (Guo et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026), by constraints and mixtures of
judges (Moskovitz et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024), by log-sigmoid conjunction of
transformed rewards (Wang et al., 2024b), by Pareto-guided optimal transport, proposed for text-
to-image models, (Ba et al., 2026), by per-channel normalization before summation (Ichihara et al.,
2026; Liu et al., 2026), by interpolating policies fine-tuned on separate rewards (Ram´e et al., 2023) or
folding several objectives into one preference objective (Zhou et al., 2024), by decoupled advantages
for rubric integration (Tan et al., 2026), by structured reward models (Wang et al., 2024a), and
by rubric-based rewards (Gunjal et al., 2025) with randomly dropped rubric items (Yang et al.,
2026). These methods balance or combine reward signals; we ask whether the aggregation rule
itself drives optimization toward the auxiliary behavior and how to remove that incentive structurally.
Classical multi-objective RL (Roijers et al., 2013; Vamplew et al., 2008; G´abor et al., 1998; Skalse
et al., 2022a) treats the specified objectives as legitimate quantities to trade off; here degradation is
measured against an unknown true reward, so Pareto optimality among the channels is not sufficient.
Reward auditing and reward selection. Flawed reward behavior is mitigated or audited with
reward-model ensembles (Coste et al., 2024) or weight-averaged reward models (Ram´e et al., 2024),
which share systematic errors and so mitigate but do not eliminate hacking (Eisenstein et al., 2024),
with adversarial auditing during training (Beigi et al., 2026), or by monitoring the chain-of-thought
during training (Baker et al., 2025). None of these attributes a behavior to a channel. Anchor
election sits earlier in the pipeline: it probes each channel alone before joint training and ranks
anchor candidates by the drift a channel induces on its own.

Reward hacking.
Skalse et al. (2022b) define reward hacking as a relation between a proxy reward
R and the true reward R⋆on a policy set Π, where the corresponding expected return with any policy
π ∈Π is J(π) = Ex∼D, y∼π(·|x)[R(x, y)] and J⋆(π) := Ex∼D, y∼π(·|x)[R⋆(x, y)], respectively.
Definition 1 (Hackable reward pair). A pair of reward functions (R, R⋆) is hackable relative to
a policy set Π if there exist π, π′ ∈Π such that J(π) < J(π′) and J⋆(π) > J⋆(π′); else it is
unhackable.

Why symmetric rules fail.
A symmetric rule reinforces the auxiliary-favoring action a1 faster
than the primary-favoring action a2, and on-policy sampling compounds its lead, an on-policy ana-
logue of shortcut learning (Geirhos et al., 2020). The policy therefore collapses onto the easier
channel, which in our settings is never correctness, the harder and slower criterion; whenever the
true reward prefers the primary action (the table above), the collapse is reward hacking. The nor-
malized and constrained variants of Section 6 fare no better, so a remedy that keeps the auxiliary
signal has to break the symmetry (Section 5).
5
BREAKING THE SYMMETRY WITH TRUST-ANCHORED GATING
The preceding analysis establishes a fundamental failure mode of symmetric aggregation: when
auxiliary rewards are easier to obtain, GRPO converges to the auxiliary-favoring behavior, even
when the primary objective is preferred (Theorem 1). Addressing this failure raises two questions:
which reward channel should take priority, and how should that priority be enforced? We propose
Trust-Anchored Gating (TAG), which first elects an anchor based on the policy drift induced by op-
timizing each channel independently (§5.1), then conditions auxiliary credit on anchor satisfaction
(§5.2). We prove that, when the primary channel is selected as the anchor, TAG reverses the selec-
tion and guarantees convergence to the primary-favoring action from any initialization (Theorem 2).

EXPERIMENTS
6.1
SETUP
Reward channels.
We test the failure of §4 and the defense of §5 in RL post-training with two
reward channels whose compatibility is known by construction. The primary channel RP is always
correctness, a gold-answer check in mathematics and unit tests in program synthesis; the auxiliary
channel RA is a synthetic signal. In compatible settings some correct responses show the auxiliary
behavior (η > 0 in §3.3); in incompatible settings none does (η = 0) and the auxiliary credit is
a grader loophole in the sense of Wang et al. (2026). Settings are named by the pair of rewarded
behaviors: Correct & brief and Correct & negative on mathematics (§6.2), Correct & fast and Correct
& no-op on program synthesis (§6.3), with RA defined in those sections.
Models.
The main grid trains Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Qwen3-4B below)
(Yang et al., 2025); §6.5 adds Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen3-
30B-A3B.
Data and evaluation.
Mathematics arms train on the Big-Math hard split (Albalak et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2026); program-synthesis arms train on DeepCoder-RT, a runtime-filtered pool built
from DeepCoder (Luo et al., 2025) (§6.3, Appendix C.4). We report held-out pass@4 and the rate
of the auxiliary behavior at the end of training and for the base model. An arm has collapsed when
the auxiliary behavior takes over and its held-out correctness falls far below the RP-only reference;
shaded table cells mark pass@4 below the base model. Appendix C (Table 6) lists the configuration.
Baselines.
All arms start from the same base model with the same data and training budget and
differ only in how the two channels enter training. Training on RP alone is the reference. Reward-
level rules shape a scalar reward and train it with standard GRPO: the additive rule (RP+RA)/2
(labeled RP+RA in the figures; the scale is immaterial under the group z-score), the weighted sum
0.9RP+0.1RA, the AND rule min(RP, RA), a Lagrangian port of Moskovitz et al. (2024) and a
reward-level port of PG-OT (Ba et al., 2026). Advantage-level estimators keep the additive reward
and change the advantage: MO-GRPO (Ichihara et al., 2026) sums per-channel group z-scores, and
Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) drops the group-standard-deviation of the GRPO loss. TAG uses Eq. (6)
with λ = 1 and the anchor elected by the probes of §5.1.

6.4
ISOLATED DRIFT AND ANCHOR ELECTION
Every probe is trained for T = 300 GRPO steps from the base model (rollout batch 32, n = 8),
and its per-token reverse KL is estimated on 256 held-out prompts at temperature 1. The probes
separate without an oracle (Figure 7): on both Qwen models the auxiliary probe drifts 2.8–23×
further than its matched correctness probe in per-token reverse KL, and 12–21× under Correct &
fast and Correct & no-op on program synthesis, so the rule elects correctness in every cell, and
every TAG arm of Tables 1 and 2 uses this elected anchor. The direction of the divergence carries
the signal, as Lemma B.1 predicts: the forward KL fails on mathematics (0.2–2.8×, and inverted
on Qwen3-4B, where brevity scores 0.15 against 0.68 for correctness) and is two to three times less
sharp on program synthesis (5–13× against 12–21×; Table 4), because base-generated text never
visits an invented mode.

CONCLUSION
Combining individually meaningful rewards can undermine LLM post-training: the aggregate ob-
jective may improve even as primary-task performance deteriorates. We identify a fundamental
mechanism behind this failure: in a stochastic decision model, symmetric aggregation that rewards
single-channel success drives GRPO toward auxiliary-favoring behavior when auxiliary rewards are
easier to obtain, regardless of initialization. To address this, we propose Trust-Anchored Gating
(TAG), which elects an anchor through isolated policy drift and structurally conditions auxiliary
credit on anchor satisfaction. When the primary channel is anchored, TAG provably reverses this
selection while preserving incentives for joint success. Experiments across mathematics and pro-
gram synthesis demonstrate that aggregation-induced failures extend beyond symmetric rules, while
TAG substantially mitigates these failures and retains the benefits of compatible auxiliary rewards.
Limitations.
TAG assumes that one reward channel is the primary objective and the others are
auxiliary, so that a single anchor can be elected; handling several primary channels, or none that
deserves priority, is a direction for future work. Anchor election relies on short, channel-isolated
probes, which introduce additional computational cost, and low policy drift does not guarantee
alignment with the unknown true reward. Finally, our theoretical guarantees are established in a
simplified two-channel, binary-reward model. Extending the analysis to more general reward set-
tings and scaling anchor election to larger reward sets are further directions.
ETHICS STATEMENT
This work studies reward hacking in order to prevent it. All auxiliary channels in our experiments
are synthetic and injected into controlled training runs; no deployed system was attacked, and no
human subjects were involved. The collapse mechanisms we characterize are already documented
in the public literature we cite; our contribution is defensive.

B
ANCHOR ELECTION: DETAILS, THEORY AND DIAGNOSTICS
B.1
METHOD DETAILS
Why probe offline.
Once rewards are optimized jointly, a behavior created by one channel can
simultaneously alter several others, making its source difficult to identify. Probing each channel in
isolation from the same base policy π0 exposes the behavior directly induced by that channel before
interactions among reward channels are introduced during joint training. The probe policies πT,k
are discarded afterwards and joint optimization restarts from π0.
Direction of the log-ratio.
The quantity KLrev
T,k of Section 5.1 evaluates the log-ratio under re-
sponses generated by the probed policy πT,k. The behavior of interest is the one created by isolated
optimization, so the divergence is evaluated under the policy that produces it. The opposite direction,
DKL(π0 ∥πT,k), evaluates behavior sampled from the base policy and can be much less sensitive
to newly created, low-base-probability response modes; Appendix B.2 (Lemma B.1) makes this
asymmetry precise for mode invention and notes that a location drift of the bulk, for which the two
divergences coincide, does not produce it, whereas an entropy change separates the two divergences
without any mode being invented.
Per-token normalization.
Without the normalization, the summed log-ratio averaged over re-
sponses is the sequence-level reverse KL DKL(πT,k ∥π0) by the chain rule. Dividing it by the
expected number of generated tokens (in the estimate, pooling the per-token log-ratios of all re-
sponses and taking their mean) gives the sequence-level reverse KL per generated token: a scale
normalization that keeps probes with different response lengths comparable.
Election rule.
Reverse-KL drift is the election signal: the channel with the smallest KLrev
T,k is
elected as the anchor. In every setting of this paper the correctness channel has the smallest drift
(Figure 7).
Scope.
The election assumes that at least one channel admits no low-base-probability shortcut.
The procedure ranks channels by how far their isolated optimization moves the policy into low-
base-probability modes (Lemma B.1). In the three-action model a saturated probe’s reverse drift
equals −log π0(aj | x), so the election picks the channel whose isolated optimum is the more
typical under π0; this coincides with the primary channel when the auxiliary action a1 is the rarer
one. Theorem 2 is stated for the case in which the elected anchor is the primary channel, as in every
setting of this paper.

δ2/2 is insensitive to where the invented mass went, while KLrev ≈ε(ℓ−1) with ℓ= log ε+∆2/2
the per-sample log-ratio of the invented mode, so KLrev/KLfwd ≈ℓ−1 grows without bound in
∆2 (Figure 8; the approximation is within 5% for ∆≥5 at ε = 0.05). At ε = 5%, ∆= 6:
KLfwd = 0.0505, KLrev = 0.704 (14×); both scale linearly with ε, so a quoted ratio should
carry its (ε, ∆). The two directions are complementary rather than redundant: for mode dropping
(bimodal base, policy keeps one mode) the ratio reverses (KLfwd = 8.31, KLrev = 0.689). KLfwd
is the forgetting monitor and KLrev the invention monitor; the collapsed behaviors in this paper
(instant short answers, negative constant answers, trivial programs that exit at once) are inventions.
Detection under benign drift. With the invented mode switched on or off at fixed ∆= 6, ε = 5%
and a benign drift δ, the KLfwd excess is “ε on top of δ2/2” and is swamped once δ ≳0.5, while the
KLrev excess is “ε(ℓ−1) on top of δ2/2” (Table 5); over a (δ, ε) grid the z ≥5 alarm region covers
22% of the grid for KLfwd and 63% for KLrev. Benign location drift pulls the ratio KLrev/KLfwd
from ℓ−1 toward 1; only a new mode pulls it away from 1 (a change of the policy’s entropy would
also do so; we consider location drift only), and the difference KLrev −KLfwd ≈ε(ℓ−2) is nearly
drift-invariant for δ ≤1 (0.65 at δ ≤0.5, 0.63 at δ = 1). The empirical counterparts are in §6.4.

and feeds it to the standard GRPO advantage unless stated otherwise. Additive: (RP+RA)/2. AND:
min(RA, RP). LSC soft-AND (Wang et al., 2024b): log σ(a(RA −c)) + log σ(a(RP −c)) with
a = 4, c = 1
2; on binary channels each term takes two values, so the shaped reward is an affine
function of RA + RP, and since the group z-score is affine-invariant the advantages coincide with
the additive rule sample by sample. Its runs reproduce the additive arm within seed noise (Tables 7
and 11) and are kept as this check; the row is omitted from Table 1. Lagrangian (Moskovitz et al.,
2024): RP + νtRA, the multiplier updated once per rollout batch by dual ascent on the constraint
E[RA] ≥τ, νt+1 = max{0, νt + αdual(τ −¯
RA)} with τ = 0.5, αdual = 0.05, ν0 = 0.5; once the
auxiliary rate exceeds τ the multiplier decays to 0 within about twenty steps and the arm continues on
RP alone. PG-OT (Ba et al., 2026): a reward-level port of the transport objective in its ε →0 limit:
per group, each channel is standardized, the Pareto front of the n points is computed, and a sample’s
shaped reward is minus its squared distance to the nearest front point (front points score 0). MO-
GRPO (Ichihara et al., 2026): the per-channel group z-scores are summed and used as the advantage

by the constant 1/Lmax, implemented by scaling Ai by |oi|/Lmax. Rubric Dropout (Yang et al.,
2026): with probability 0.5 per group the auxiliary channel is dropped and the group is scored by
RP alone, otherwise by (RA + RP)/2. Weights are equal except in the weighted-sum arm; under
the z-score a weight is immaterial in two-level groups (Appendix A.1). Additive+KL: the additive

PROGRAM SYNTHESIS
Protocol. DeepCoder-RT is built from DeepCoder problems with one large-input test appended; a
problem is kept if its human reference solution passes all tests and needs at least 10 ms of CPU time
on the large input (3,741 train / 185 held-out); on the held-out set no correct program of any arm
exits within 3 ms. RP = 1 iff all tests pass in a sandbox (isolated python3 -I subprocess per
test; 10 s per small test, 30 s for the mid-size test, the large-input test last and only for programs
that passed everything before it). T is the largest CPU time over the executed tests, large input
included. Training: GRPO with n = 8, rollout batch 256, 500 steps, learning rate 10−6, no KL,
clip 0.2/0.28, response cap 4096; the auxiliary channels are RA = 1[T < 60 ms] (Correct & fast)
and RA = 1[T < 3 ms] (Correct & no-op), judged online during training. Evaluation: n = 8
samples per held-out problem are regenerated from the final checkpoint (temperature 1, cap 4096)
and all programs of all arms are re-executed in a single session on one node (AMD EPYC 7R13,
64 concurrent programs), so every latency in Table 2 shares one clock; the reference solutions are
timed under the same judge, and “fast” in the table means T < Tref. Shaded cells in Table 2 mark
pass@4 below the base model (collapse as defined in §6.1); pass@4 is the unbiased estimate from
the n = 8 samples; latencies exclude timeouts.
