#### ABSTRACT

Long-horizon language-model agents trained with reinforcement learning often
receive sparse outcome rewards that do not reveal which decisions along a tra-
jectory deserve credit. Episode-level advantages provide coarse trajectory-wide
credit, while step-level comparisons offer finer resolution with context-dependent
estimation noise. We propose Granularity-Adaptive Credit Assignment (GACA),
a critic-free method that adaptively mixes episode- and step-level credit for each
decision during policy optimization. GACA normalizes the sampled response’s
mean per-token negative log-likelihood (NLL) within each trajectory and uses the
resulting criticality score to determine the step-specific mixture. The computation
reuses rollout log-probabilities without additional training rollouts or model eval-
uations. Our analysis characterizes optimal score-dependent mixing and derives
conditions linking expected NLL to a lower bound on the preferred step-level
weight. Across ALFWorld and WebShop with 1.5B and 7B backbones, GACA
achieves the highest reported mean success rates among the compared methods,
while introducing negligible additional computation.

#### 1


#### INTRODUCTION

Language-model agents act over many steps to complete household tasks, browse web stores, and
use external tools (Yao et al., 2022b; Shridhar et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2022a). Reinforcement learning
can improve these agents using task outcomes (Ouyang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025), but a sparse
terminal reward leaves a difficult credit-assignment problem: which decisions should receive the
learning signal? A trajectory can contain both consequential choices and actions whose alternatives
would have little effect on the outcome (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019).
Critic-free methods avoid training a value model by comparing several rollouts of the same task.
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) computes an episode-level relative advantage and broadcasts it to every
action. GiGPO (Feng et al., 2026) adds local resolution by grouping steps with the same anchor
observation and comparing their continuation returns. Its fixed combination of episode- and step-
level signals leaves a further question: should every decision rely on these two credit estimates in
the same proportion?
The two granularities offer a decision-dependent estimation trade-off. Episode credit spreads the
outcome over all decisions, while step credit separates local continuations using finite, context-
dependent comparison groups.
We propose Granularity-Adaptive Credit Assignment (GACA),
which adapts their relative contribution during policy optimization.
For each completed rollout, GACA computes the sampled response’s mean per-token negative log-
likelihood (NLL) from old-policy log-probabilities. Dividing by the trajectory mean gives a critical-
ity score that measures how surprising the response is relative to other responses in that interaction.

A bounded mapping converts this score into a step-specific mixing weight, placing more weight
on step-level credit at higher scores. These computations reuse quantities already available for the
policy update, requiring no additional model or training rollouts.
Our analysis characterizes how a score-dependent mixture balances the errors of the two credit
estimates. It then identifies conditions under which greater variation in action values favors step-
level credit and expected NLL provides a lower bound on that variation. These results motivate
increasing the weight of local credit with the NLL-based score.
GACA complements methods that refine the step estimator, including history-consistent grouping
in HGPO (He et al., 2026), behavior-aware grouping in BiPACE (Wang et al., 2026a), and progress-
conditioned credit in ProGPO (Yang et al., 2026). Uncertainty-based modulation in AEM and
STARE rescales an existing advantage (Zhao et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026); GACA uses uncer-
tainty to control the relative contribution of two credit granularities. Appendix A discusses these
connections.
Our evaluation covers ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen2.5-Instruct at 1.5B and 7B scales. On
ALFWorld 1.5B, matched ablations compare GACA with five fixed mixtures and within-trajectory
random score assignment. Adaptive mixing reaches 96.88% success, compared with 93.05% for the
best tested fixed mixture and 88.54% for random assignment. These comparisons examine both the
choice of mixing weights and their assignment to individual decisions.

#### 4


#### EXPERIMENTS

Our evaluation measures attained task success on two agent benchmarks, progress during training,
and per-update computational cost. Matched ablations examine stepwise credit allocation, followed
by diagnostics of the NLL signal.
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Tasks and training.
We use the verl-agent stack (Feng et al., 2026; Sheng et al., 2025) with
Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B and 7B. ALFWorld involves household tasks with a maximum of 50 steps;
WebShop involves product search and purchase within 15 steps. GACA samples groups of 8 trajec-
tories and applies an invalid-action penalty of 0.1. For ALFWorld 1.5B, we use a two-step history
window, rollout temperature 1, and 16 task groups per update. We train this model for 300 parameter
updates. We report its update-150, best, and final evaluations to distinguish performance at a shorter
budget from gains under continued training. Appendix D gives training settings and benchmark
details.

4.3
EFFECT OF ADAPTIVE CREDIT ALLOCATION
We use ALFWorld 1.5B to test three aspects of credit allocation: combining episode and step signals,
adapting their mixture beyond a constant weight, and assigning the resulting weights to individual
decisions. Figure 3 presents the fixed-weight sweep and random assignment control. The historical
step-weighted mean is ¯λtrain ≈0.748, motivating fixed λ = 0.75 as a near-mean comparator.
Appendix G gives the complete numerical grid.
Adaptive versus fixed mixing.
With adaptation disabled (αλ = 0), we vary the constant weight
over {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1}. The episode-only and step-only endpoints reach 85.94% and 84.60%,
while every tested interior mixture performs better than both endpoints. Combining the two granu-
larities therefore helps even with a fixed weight. The best tested constant, λ = 0.5, reaches 93.05%;
fixed λ = 0.75, close to GACA’s mean weight, reaches 92.41%. With the same episode- and
step-level estimators, GACA reaches 96.88%, exceeding these controls by 3.83 and 4.47 points,
respectively. The results support adapting the relative contribution of the two credit signals across
decisions.
Does the assignment to steps matter?
Randomly permuting criticality scores within each trajec-
tory reduces success from 96.88% to 88.54%. This control retains the trajectory’s collection of gate
values and changes their pairing with the episode and step advantages. The 8.34-point drop shows
that performance depends on where the weights are assigned. Together with the fixed-weight sweep,
it supports using the score to allocate the two credit signals at individual decisions.
The realized step weights span 0.623–0.900 at the 10th–90th percentiles in frozen-policy rollouts;
Appendix H.1 gives their distribution and the training mean.

We examine whether the score used by GACA identifies decisions with greater action diversity

training performance and that higher weights are associated with more action-diverse decisions.

#### CONCLUSION

GACA uses rollout NLL to adapt the mixture of episode- and step-level credit during policy opti-
mization, without additional training rollouts or model evaluations. Conditional risk analysis char-
acterizes score-dependent mixing and conditions favoring finer credit. Across ALFWorld and Web-
Shop with two backbone sizes, GACA achieves the highest reported mean success rates among
the compared methods. In matched ALFWorld ablations, GACA improves over the tested fixed
weights and within-trajectory random assignment. Together, these results support step-dependent
credit mixing as a practical design choice for critic-free agent training.

#### A


#### EXTENDED RELATED WORK

Critic-free RL for LLMs and agents. Replacing the learned value function with a group baseline
has become a common recipe for RL on language models, from GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; Guo
et al., 2025) and REINFORCE-style variants (Ahmadian et al., 2024) to refinements of clipping
and normalization (Yu et al., 2026). Carrying this from single-turn reasoning to multi-turn agents
required new infrastructure and recipes (Sheng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). GiGPO (Feng
et al., 2026) is the direct methodological precursor from which our reported implementation inherits
anchor-observation step grouping. Subsequent work has exposed limitations of observation-only
step groups: HGPO uses hierarchical history-consistent groups to address context inconsistency (He
et al., 2026), while BiPACE replaces surface observation hashing with behavior-aware grouping and
action-conditioned peer baselines (Wang et al., 2026a). ProGPO addresses a complementary long-
horizon credit trap by introducing progress-conditioned relative advantages in failure-dominated
groups (Yang et al., 2026). These methods modify the construction of local credit estimates. GACA
addresses a complementary choice: the relative weight assigned to local and episode-level credit at
each step. The mixing analysis accommodates alternative step estimators; the reported experiments
use GiGPO-style anchor grouping.
Credit assignment and reward redistribution. Assigning delayed outcomes to individual de-
cisions is a central problem in reinforcement learning.
Approaches include return decomposi-
tion (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019), hindsight relabeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017), generalized
advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2015), and potential-based shaping (Ng et al., 1999). For
LLMs, process reward models supply dense step-level supervision where intermediate correctness is
checkable (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Uesato et al., 2022), and VinePPO replaces the
critic with Monte-Carlo value estimates from extra rollouts branched at intermediate states (Kazem-
nejad et al., 2024).
These approaches use additional process supervision or intermediate-state
rollouts. GACA reuses rollout log-probabilities to condition the mixture of existing credit esti-
mates. GAE trades bias and variance through a mixture of temporal estimates; GACA instead mixes
two critic-free group-relative estimates using a per-step score. Proposition 1 characterizes score-
dependent mixing relative to local credit, while Proposition 2 isolates a regime in which finer credit
becomes increasingly valuable as local action values separate.
Uncertainty-guided advantage modulation. Recent work also uses surprisal to reshape RL up-
dates. AEM (Zhao et al., 2026) analyzes response-level entropy dynamics in multi-turn agents and
rescales a base response-level advantage using a response-level uncertainty proxy. STARE (Luo
et al., 2026) identifies high-surprisal tokens and selectively reweights their effective advantages to
stabilize policy entropy. These methods rescale a pre-existing credit signal. GACA uses the score to
construct a mixture of episode- and step-level credit estimates.
Entropy and the value of uncertain decisions. Entropy has long shaped exploration and stability
in RL (Haarnoja et al., 2018), and recent analyses of RL for LLM reasoning show that a small, high-
entropy minority of tokens drives most of the policy improvement while entropy collapse degrades
learning (Wang et al., 2026b; Cui et al., 2025). GACA uses uncertainty at the environment-step level
to decide how much to rely on the two credit granularities.

The population analysis uses h(s) = E[Ht | s], the expected mean per-token NLL of the full
response. GACA uses one sampled Ht at each decision and computes ct = Ht/Hτ from the com-
pleted rollout. The score can therefore depend on the sampled action and subsequent continuation.
During policy optimization, scores and advantages are treated as fixed quantities, with no gradients
propagated through them. Proposition 1 allows their statistical dependence.

G.1
MODULATION STRENGTH AND THE MIXING LAW
At λ0 = 0.5, the tested strengths αλ = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1 give success rates of 93.05%, 95.12%, 96.88%,
and 95.44%. All three nonzero strengths have higher mean success than the fixed mixture, with the
default 0.5 giving the largest mean. Increasing αλ raises the weight at a given positive score and
lowers the criticality threshold for saturation. The sweep therefore varies the mean weight together
with sensitivity to criticality and the extent of clipping.
