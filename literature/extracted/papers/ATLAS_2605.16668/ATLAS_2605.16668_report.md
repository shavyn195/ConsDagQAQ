Abstract
We introduce GraViti, a transformer-based graph-level variational autoencoder that encodes en-
tire graphs into single, fixed-dimensional latent vectors rather than per-node embeddings, yielding
a graph-level latent space that supports smooth interpolation, property-guided search, and other
downstream tasks beyond the reach of node-level approaches. GraViti achieves state-of-the-art re-
construction accuracy on large molecular graph datasets while offering competitive single-step gen-
erative performance. Our central finding concerns where permutation invariance is needed in such
a model, and where it is not.
The encoder remains permutation-invariant throughout, ensuring
the model generalizes to graphs regardless of input order. The reconstruction loss, however, does
not need to be: when training data is provided in a consistent node order, comparing predictions
to targets directly in that order, without a graph-matching step, is sufficient. Relaxing invariance
in the loss alone lets GraViti avoid the cubic-cost matching step required by prior graph-level au-
toencoders, reducing complexity to quadratic while improving reconstruction fidelity. We show the
resulting latent space is chemically meaningful, supporting controlled molecular editing and property
optimization, recovering established chemical trends, and enabling direct regression of graph-level
properties from latent embeddings.

###### 1


###### Introduction

Autoencoders [1] are unsupervised learning models designed to learn compact representations of complex
data. They consist of two components: an encoder that maps an input data point (such as an image, or
in our case a graph) into a low-dimensional latent vector, and a decoder that reconstructs the original
data from this latent code. The model is trained end-to-end by minimizing the difference between the in-
put and its reconstruction. This training process forces the latent representation to capture the essential
structure of the data while discarding redundancy and noise. As a result, autoencoders are widely used
for tasks such as denoising, compression, and extracting meaningful features that can support down-
stream applications [2, 3]. To enforce structure on the latent representations, variational autoencoders
[4] constrain the distribution of all data embeddings z to match a fixed prior (usually normal) through
minimizing a KL-divergence term.
While autoencoders have been applied across various modalities [5], graph autoencoding remains
challenging due to graphs’ inherently discrete and non-euclidean nature.
Most approaches covering
graph-oriented AE and VAEs do so at node-level. The early approach by Kipf et al. [6] encodes a graph

In this work, we introduce GraViti, a graph-level VAE that encodes entire graphs into single latent
vectors using a graph-transformer backbone. Our contributions are threefold. First, we propose a scal-
able architecture that achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction accuracy on large graph datasets. Second,
we show that the learned latent space is well structured: on molecular benchmarks, it supports interpola-
tion, controlled editing, and property-driven optimization, recovers well-established chemical phenomena,
and provides competitive single-step molecular generation. Third, we show through ablations that this
improvement stems specifically from relaxing permutation invariance in the reconstruction loss, while
keeping the encoder itself permutation-invariant. This relaxation only requires that the training data
follows a consistent node ordering, not that the ordering rule itself be known, a property that holds for
many real-world graph datasets.

###### 2


###### Background and state of the art

Permutation-invariant encoding of size-varying graphs
Early GNNs learn permutation equivari-
ant node representations via message passing with permutation-invariant aggregation [13, 14], including
attention-based aggregators that allow neighbor-specific information transmission [15, 16, 17].
Since message-passing GNNs have limited expressivity [14], graph transformers were introduced,
letting all nodes attend to all others at the cost of a full attention matrix [18, 19], with positional
encodings now carrying the structural information [20, 21]. Hybrid approaches such as GraphGPS [22]
and SAT [23] combine transformers with message passing to balance efficiency and flexibility.
Both
families operate directly on sets of nodes and edges, giving permutation-equivariant representations that
handle graphs of arbitrary size without a fixed ordering, making them suitable graph encoders as in
the original Graph VAE [6]. A permutation-invariant pooling operation then produces a graph-level
representation, though often at the cost of discarding substantial structural information [24].
Size-varying and permutation-invariant decoding
Graph (variational) autoencoders must recon-
struct a graph of unknown size and determine the correspondence between reconstructed and input nodes
to compute a reconstruction loss. Node-level approaches handle this naturally: the encoder produces one
latent vector per node, and the decoder reconstructs adjacency and attributes in the same fixed order,
so correspondence is implicit in node indexing. Common decoders include inner-product or MLP heads
[6], sometimes refined via message passing [25].
Graph-level autoencoders lack this implicit ordering and must instead resolve it explicitly. Graph
matching [26, 27] finds an optimal node alignment, at the cost of solving an expensive quadratic assign-
ment problem. PIGVAE [11] instead learns a relaxed permutation matrix by sorting node embeddings,
avoiding the assignment problem but offering only limited alignment; GRALE [12] improves on this with
a Sinkhorn-based matcher and an optimal-transport reconstruction loss, yielding a full doubly stochastic
alignment matrix with better stability and expressivity.
Variable graph size is typically handled by padding to a fixed maximum, as in NLP transformers [15],
often via a “dummy node” class [12, 28], with the decoder predicting a maximal-size graph plus a node
mask. This has two drawbacks: it prevents generalization to larger graphs, and the dummy class biases
node classification with an artificial label. PIGVAE instead broadcasts the graph representation across a
sequence transformer, using sinusoidal positional encodings to differentiate duplicated tokens; this avoids
both a maximum size and a dummy class, masking only non-existent nodes, with size predicted by an
MLP from the latent.
Overall, existing methods trade off scalability against expressivity: node-level approaches scale well

###### 3


###### The GraViti architecture

Graph representation
Let N denote the size of the graph (i.e., the number of nodes). In this work, we
assume that graphs have discrete node classes and edge classes instead of continuous features (e.g. atom
classes, color). A graph is represented by two objects. First is its node-class matrix X ∈RN×nc where
nc is the number of node classes and Xi is the one-hot encoding of the i-th node’s class. Importantly,
we do not introduce a “dummy node” class; all classes correspond to real nodes. Then E ∈RN×N×ne is
the edge class tensor, where ne is the number of edge classes, including both actual edge types and an
additional class representing “no edge”.
To enrich the graph representation, we compute directly from the graph a global feature vector
y ∈Rdy containing graph-level information. In practice, dy = nc + ne + 1, and y encodes the count
of each node and edge type, as well as the graph size. We also compute P ∈RN×dP , the positional
encodings for the nodes, obtained by concatenating Laplacian positional encodings and random walk
positional encodings. These inject global structural information into the graph transformer at no extra
cost.
Finally, when relevant for the field, we also exploit a matrix of node-specific features derived from
domain knowledge K ∈RN×dk. For molecular datasets, these include atomic properties such as atomic
number, atomic mass, valence electrons, and electronegativity.
Since training involves batching, all graphs in a batch must share the same size. To achieve this, we
pad all graphs to reach the size of the largest graph in the batch, extending X, P, K, and E accordingly.
This padding has no semantic meaning, and the model is designed to ignore it entirely. The padded size
of the input does not influence the decoder or model size in any way. The model is illustrated in figure
1; the letters in paragraph titles refer to the respective components on the figure.
Encoding node, edge, and global representation (A)
Our encoder maps a triplet X, E, y (op-
tionally P and K) to a mean and standard deviation zµ, zσ ∈Rdz where dz is the latent dimension.
At first, the node representation matrix is expanded into Xemb by concatenating a learned shallow em-
bedding of the node class, both positional encoding (Laplacian and random-walk) processed through a
dedicated linear layer and optionally node attributes processed through a dedicated linear layer. Edge
representations are obtained via a learned embedding layer of their discrete classes, producing Eemb.
The global feature vector y is projected through a linear layer into yemb.
All three tensors (Xemb, Eemb, yemb) undergo layer normalization and are updated through multiple
graph transformer layers, yielding Xrep, Erep, and yrep. This step uses the specific graph transformer
architecture introduced in DiGress [29] and updated in CatFlow [30]. This design enables simultaneous
updates of node, edge, and graph-level features while ensuring permutation-equivariance.
Encoder readout (B)
Traditional readout functions for graph-level representations (such as sum,
mean, or max pooling) are permutation-invariant at the cost of a large quantity of information. To
ensure a highly expressive latent space, we adopt a sequence transformer-based readout mechanism.
Design motivations as to why we maintain a permutation-invariant encoder are detailed in Appendix A.
Unlike GRALE, which applies a transformer solely on node embeddings, our approach incorporates
nodes, edges, and global features into the readout to prevent overloading node embeddings. Specifically,
the readout consists of two parallel sequence-decoder transformers: one for nodes and one for edges. Both
transformers ignore padded nodes and edges. yrep serves as the target for both transformers, injecting
global context into the readout.
Since positional encodings are omitted at this stage, the inherent permutation invariance of the
sequence transformer guarantees that the output is independent of node ordering. Finally, node and
edge outputs are normalized independently and projected through linear layers to produce the graph-
level representation.
This one can be either a single vector (classic autoencoder case) or a pair of
same-sized vectors (in the variational case).

###### 4


###### Comparison to GRALE and PIGVAE

Loss function Both GRALE and PIGVAE leverage a permutation-invariant scheme for their loss func-
tions, allowing to reconstruct the graph in any order while still obtaining a relevant reconstruction loss:
PIGVAE predicts a permutation matrix obtained through a solver, while GRALE iterates upon this by
using a Sinkhorn matcher to obtain a relaxed permutation matrix, and using the Any2Graph loss [31] on
the soft assignment. While these approaches are elegant and well-suited to fully general graph settings,
this incurs a significant computational overhead (at least cubic for both approaches). In contrast, our
model relies on a simpler reconstruction objective made possible by the use of a consistent node order-
ing, allowing a quadratic complexity. In practice, this results improved reconstruction quality on the
benchmarks we consider, especially as graph size increases.
Graph size flexibility GRALE decodes graphs by iteratively refining a learned node and edge
representation of fixed maximal size. This mechanism inherently enforces an upper bound on the size of
generated graphs. In contrast, our approach, like PIGVAE, does not impose such a limit: the graph-level
latent is replicated to the desired number of nodes, and continuous sequence-based positional encodings
are used to distinguish node-level embeddings. As a result, the model architecture is independent of
graph size, although computational cost for full attention naturally scales with it.
Variational component Similar to PIGVAE, our method follows a fully variational formulation,
enabling likelihood-based training and explicit regularization of the latent space. Unlike GRALE, which
relies on a tokenized latent representation, we operate in a continuous latent space. In practice, this
enables a well-structured latent, unlocking in particular generative and editing capabilities.

###### Experimental results

Experimental setting
A prominent application domain for graph-level representation learning is
computational chemistry, where molecules are naturally represented as attributed graphs of atoms and
bonds. We consider two benchmark datasets: QM9 [32], a dataset of approximately 130,000 molecules
containing up to 9 heavy atoms, and PubChem [33]. Following GRALE, we focus on the PubChem16 and
PubChem32 subsets, comprising approximately 13 million and 74 million molecules with up to 16 and
32 heavy atoms, respectively. Both datasets are publicly provided in a canonical ordering established
by proprietary softwares, as detailed in Appendix B alongside the dataset statistics. As is standard
in molecular graph modeling, we remove explicit hydrogen atoms [34, 35, 30, 36] and study both the
aromatic form and the kekulized form.
The resulting graphs contain three bond types (resp.
four)
in kekulized form (resp. aromatic): single, double, triple (resp. and aromatic). An additional edge

Networks-Asia dataset, which consists in directed acyclic graphs.
Training settings can be found in
Appendix C.
Reconstruction performance
Table 1 reports the graph reconstruction performance of our model
using what we consider to be the primary evaluation metric of this study. We deliberately adopt a
particularly strict reconstruction criterion, under which a graph is deemed correctly reconstructed only
if all node types and all edge types are predicted without error. For molecules in aromatic form, this
requirement is further extended to include the exact reconstruction of the hydrogen count and formal
charge for every aromatic atom. The reported accuracy therefore corresponds to the proportion of graphs
that are perfectly reconstructed. We also report the less strict edit distance [37] for each model. All
models are trained until convergence.
We observe that our model outperforms the matching-based baselines on all molecular datasets. We
also notice that, as the size of the graphs in the datasets increases, so does the performance gap, with our
model beating the baselines by 25% on PubChem32. We attribute this success to the increasing difficulty
of training a proper matching as graph size increases for GRALE. This demonstrates in particular that the
canonical ordering provided in molecular datasets is sufficiently consistent to ensure good performance,
especially as graphs get larger, rejoining results observed on readout functions [38]. Results in aromatic
form are similar (see Appendix D). The ASIA-BN dataset was perfectly fitted by all models.

Performance and efficiency
On top of the higher performance ceiling, we further demonstrate the
efficiency gains obtained by leveraging a canonical ordering. To this end, we train two models of com-
parable size, one GRALE and one GraViti, for the same number of epochs. The results, reported in
Table 2, show that eliminating the matching step leads to a substantial reduction in training time while
simultaneously yielding higher reconstruction accuracy. These gains become even more pronounced as
graph size increases.
Ablation studies
To determine whether our gains stem from architectural choices or from the canon-
ical ordering used in the loss, we ablate several components of the architecture (Table 3). The model
proves remarkably robust to most modifications.
The largest drop comes from replacing the trans-
former encoder with a message-passing GNN, though this variant still outperforms GRALE. Training
with GRALE’s matching-based loss instead, however, yields 96.5% accuracy, matching GRALE’s 96.9%,
which strongly suggests that the reconstruction objective accounts for most of the improvement.
Limitations of Canonical Ordering Dependance
To illustrate the limits of our scope, we test on
COLORING, a very general dataset with no natural node ordering, where our model should struggle. As
expected, baseline reconstruction accuracy is near 0%. Imposing a generic, domain-agnostic WL-color-
based node ordering (details in Appendix F) raises accuracy to 80.5%, while remaining significantly
below matching-based models. Table 4 reports the full results alongside comparisons to matching-based
baselines. This shows a middle ground between no ordering at all, where our model fails outright, and
the well-informed, domain-specific canonical orderings available for molecules, where our model achieves
state-of-the-art performance.
Interpolation
We also demonstrate that the latent embedding enables smooth transition between two
molecules. To illustrate this, we perform linear and rotative interpolation directly in latent space between
the embeddings of two valid molecules. This produces a continuous trajectory of latent vectors, each of
which we decode back into a molecular graph. Because the decoder may map nearby latent points to
identical structures, we retain only the distinct molecules encountered along the interpolation. Example
interpolations sequences are shown in Figure 2, with more in Appendix J.

Graph generation capability
While molecular graph generation is not the main focus of this work,
it arises naturally from the architecture of a VAE. In particular, molecules can be generated in a single
decoder pass by sampling the latent prior. We report generation results mainly as a diagnostic of the
learned latent space and decoder, rather than as an attempt to optimize a dedicated molecular generator
or establish a new state of the art, especially as VAEs are generally not competitive compared to more
sophisticated, multi-step approaches such as diffusion [29] or flow matching [49].
Table 5 reports performance on QM9 and PubChem16 in terms of validity, uniqueness, novelty, and
Frechet ChemNet Distance [50]. Despite using no chemistry-specific constraints during decoding, the non-
aromatic QM9 model achieves 93.18% validity and 98.48% uniqueness. These results are competitive with
recent methods specifically designed for one-step generation. For example, one-step Categorical Flow
Maps [48] reports 95.8% validity and 89.2% uniqueness with its ECLD objective, and 90.0% validity and
91.8% uniqueness with its CSD objective. Our model therefore reaches a comparable validity regime
while producing a larger fraction of unique samples.
Using explicit aromatic representation, we obtain 46.5% validity on PubChem16 and 92% on QM9.
A large fraction of invalid molecules contain aromatic edges not forming closed aromatic cycles, reflecting
the known difficulty of graph neural networks, including transformers, in representing cyclic structures
without higher-order mechanisms [51, 52, 53]. Training the model on the corresponding kekulized rep-

###### 6


###### Conclusion and future work

We introduced GraViti, a transformer-based graph-level variational autoencoder that learns compact
latent representations of entire graphs and achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction accuracy among graph-
level VAEs on large graph datasets.
We demonstrate on molecular graphs that the latent space is coherent and chemically meaningful:
it supports smooth interpolation, denoising, and property-guided optimization, even recovering known
chemical principles. Beyond molecular tasks, we also obtain strong performance on downstream inference
problems for Bayesian graphs.
Finally, we show that a permutation invariant objective is not always required; when a stable node
ordering is available, relaxing this constraint yields substantial gains in reconstruction quality and scala-
bility. Future work includes integrating domain-aware constraints to improve generation validity, explor-
ing richer latent priors, and evaluating transfer to other graph domains such as circuits, proteins, and
knowledge graphs. Overall, GraViti offers a practical foundation for graph-level representation learning
and provides a usable latent space for downstream tasks.

###### Design choices


###### A.1


###### Graph-level or Node-level

To motivate our use of a graph-level encoder (despite leveraging a canonical ordering), we implemented a
node-level version of our VAE, where the latent space consists of per-atom embeddings. The architecture
is the same as the main paper, but the readout and readin are removed. While this variant achieves
higher reconstruction performance (99.94% accuracy, 0.004 edit distance), its generative performance
collapses: only 17% of sampled molecules are valid, compared to 68% for our graph-level VAE.
This outcome is consistent with prior work showing that node-wise generative models struggle to
enforce global chemical constraints. GraphVAE reports at best 57% validity on QM9 [26] (with the
exception of a NoGM reaching 81%, but due to a collapse on a mode, leading to only 24% uniqueness).
MolGAN [61] also report that no baseline exceeds 57% on QM9 (with the exception of NoGM), and
uses an extensive reinforcement learning procedure specifically to improve validity. More recent analyses
demonstrate that node-level likelihoods fundamentally fail to capture global structural dependencies,
with graph-level modeling significantly improving validity: “It is known from previous work that node-
level representations, while useful for many applications, do not capture enough graph structure to
generate realistic graphs” [7].
In particular, the basic setting of sampling each node independently
prevents sampling a globally coherent molecule with high probability.
Node-level embeddings also break down outside the generation task. Because their dimensionality
depends on the number of atoms, they live in different latent spaces for different molecules, so there
is no canonical way to define distances or interpolate between them. This makes latent-space editing,
smooth interpolation, and property-guided traversal ill-posed: one cannot move “between” molecules
when their latent representations have incompatible shapes. In the same way, optimization algorithms
such as CMA-ES work on fixed-sized spaces. Graph-level embeddings avoid this entirely by mapping
every molecule into a single fixed-dimensional latent space where distances, directions, and edits are well
defined, regardless of size.

###### B.2


###### Canonizations

All datasets below are publicly provided in canonized form, and were used as-is.
QM9 [32] is a subset of a larger database, GDB-17. GDB-171 [62] is stored as canonized SMILES
strings.
The canonization of GDB-17’s SMILES representations is documented in the primary enumeration
paper [62]. The methods section states that all software was implemented using JChem libraries from the
ChemAxon proprietary software, and that each generated molecule was converted to a unique SMILES
string specifically to detect and remove duplicates before storage. Note that the paper uses the term
“unique” rather than “canonical” when describing this step.
PubChem’s canonization applies directly to atom ordering rather than only to an upstream SMILES
string. PubChem’s standardization pipeline invokes OpenEye’s OECanonicalOrderAtoms as part of its
aromaticity and kekulized form standardization2 [63]. The resulting canonical SMILES strings are parsed
with RDKit’s Chem.MolFromSmiles function, which preserves the input order rather than recomputing
its own canonization, so the canonical ordering established by OpenEye is retained in the molecules we
construct.
Neither OpenEye’s OEChem nor ChemAxon’s JChem are open source, so we do not have access to the
specific canonization algorithms in either case. This is not an obstacle in our case thanks to the encoder
being permutation invariant. As long as the training dataset’s ordering remains consistent (as it is), the
model functions well on any new molecule, regardless of ordering, so long as no permutation-sensitive
loss is required.
