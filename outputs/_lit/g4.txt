16:Recent advances have demonstrated the effectiveness of self-evolving LLM agents on tasks such as program
17:repair and scientific discovery. In this paradigm, a planner LLM continuously synthesizes agent programs that
18:invoke parametric components, including generative models such as LLMs, and fine-tunes them. However,
19:existing work has shown that self-evolving agent frameworks can exhibit undesirable behavior on unseen
20:inputs. Because these synthesized programs are often executed autonomously, the lack of formal guarantees
21:that hold for all inputs raises serious reliability and security concerns. To address this gap, we introduce
22:specification-driven self-evolving agent synthesis, which combines formal behavioral specifications that the
23:synthesized agents must satisfy on all inputs with best-effort soft objectives that capture task utility. We
24:propose Formally Guarded Generative Models (FGGM), which allow the planner LLM to bind each generative-
25:model call to a formal output contract. Each FGGM call automatically wraps the underlying parametric
26:generative model within a stateful rejection sampler and a verified fallback, treating model outputs as samples
27:from a proposal distribution. Consequently, every returned output satisfies the specified contract for any input
28:and any parameter setting of the underlying model. Building on FGGM, we present SEVerA (Self-Evolving
29:Verified Agents), a four-stage framework for specification-driven agent synthesis. In the Ideation stage, the
30:planner LLM proposes task-specific generative components by constructing FGGMs. In the Search stage, the
31:planner LLM synthesizes candidate parametric programs containing multiple FGGM components. In the
32:Verification stage, we prove correctness with respect to the hard constraints for all parameter values, thereby
33:reducing the remaining optimization problem to unconstrained learning. Finally, in the Learning stage, we
34:apply scalable gradient-based optimization, including GRPO-style fine-tuning for LLMs, to optimize the soft

1793:Conclusion
1794:We presented SEVerA, a framework for specification-driven synthesis of self-evolving LLM agents
1795:with formal behavioral guarantees. Its core abstraction, the Formally Guarded Generative Model
1796:(FGGM), binds each generative-model call to a Hoare-style local contract enforced by a stateful
1797:rejection sampler with a verified fallback. Because the fallback discharges the contract for every
1798:parameter value, correctness is parameter-independent and survives arbitrary fine-tuning. This
1799:lets SEVerA split the constrained learning problem into four stages (ideation, search, verification,
1800:and learning), so that scalable methods such as GRPO can optimize task reward without putting
1801:the specification at risk. We proved that SEVerA is sound, gave a sufficient condition under which
1802:a verified agent matches or exceeds the reward of the best unconstrained model, and showed
1803:that FGGMs retain the expressivity of the underlying transformer. Across program verification,
1804:symbolic math synthesis, policy-compliant tool use, and constrained symbolic regression, SEVerA
1805:attains zero constraint violations on held-out inputs while improving task performance over both
1806:unconstrained frameworks and task-specific state of the art. These results suggest that behavioral
1807:specifications play a dual role: certifying compliance on all inputs and, by pruning shortcut-laden
1808:candidates, steering evolution toward higher-utility agents.

23:Abstract
24:In structured decision-making workflows such as form filling, compliance check-
25:ing, and maintenance reporting, LLM outputs must be locally correct, globally
26:consistent, and auditable against task-specific rules. Existing refinement methods
27:often rely on heuristic debate, self-play, or LLM-generated supervision, creating a
28:second-order assurance problem. We propose DPA-GRPO (Dual Paired-Action
29:Group-Relative Policy Optimization), a paired-action training method for a two-
30:player generator–verifier game with structured verifier interventions. The generator
31:proposes outputs and may revise them when challenged; the verifier either remains
32:silent or raises a safety assurance case (SAC) containing a claim, argument, and
33:evidence. These SAC/no-SAC and KEEP/REVISE decisions induce paired coun-
34:terfactual action groups, which DPA-GRPO uses for role-specific KL-regularized
35:GRPO updates. We analyze the unregularized game and show that positive proba-
36:bility on strictly lower-reward intervention or revision actions creates a profitable
37:unilateral deviation. Under standard stochastic-approximation assumptions, DPA-
38:GRPO tracks the corresponding game ODE, whose isolated asymptotically stable
39:limit points are stationary and candidate local equilibria under role-wise local
40:optimality. Experiments on TaxCalcBench TY24 show that DPA-GRPO improves
41:structured decision accuracy over zero-shot generation and generator-only RL
42:baselines across Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B. Training increases correct silent accep-
43:tance, reduces missed errors, and improves calibrated revision behavior, indicating
44:gains for both generator and verifier.

990:Limitations and Broader Impact
991:DPA-GRPO is evaluated on TaxCalcBench TY24, a controlled benchmark with deterministic ground-
992:truth Form 1040 outputs. This setting is useful for studying verifiable structured decisions, but future
993:work should evaluate the method on additional domains with noisier, incomplete, or partially subjec-
994:tive supervision. Our theoretical analysis is local and relies on standard stochastic-approximation
995:and game-theoretic assumptions; it does not imply global convergence or guarantee that finite-step
996:LLM training reaches a Nash equilibrium. Empirically, performance may depend on model capacity,
997:reward design, SAC quality, and the frequency of useful revision opportunities.
998:The method also incurs additional computational cost relative to generator-only training because it
999:trains both a generator and a verifier and constructs paired counterfactual action groups. While LoRA
1000:adapters reduce this cost, scaling to larger models and longer workflows may require more efficient
1001:sampling and verifier-scoring strategies. From a broader-impact perspective, SACs can improve
1002:auditability by exposing claim-argument-evidence records, but they should not be interpreted as
1003:formal guarantees of correctness or safety. In high-stakes settings, outputs should remain subject to
1004:expert review and independent validation.

767:Conclusion
768:We proposed DPA-GRPO, a two-player generator-verifier framework for verifiable assurance reason-

18:Abstract
19:Agentic reinforcement learning (Agentic RL) has achieved strong progress in tasks
20:with clear success signals. However, many real-world agent applications require
21:user-conditioned behavior: the same query may call for different planning strategies
22:and tool-use decisions across users. This setting raises key challenges: generic
23:rewards cannot capture heterogeneous user preferences, observed behaviors are
24:entangled with conformity effects, and flat memories cannot support personal-
25:ized skill retrieval. To this end, we propose a unified personalized Agentic RL
26:framework that embeds personalization into training-time optimization. At its
27:core is Personalized Anchor Reward-Decoupled Policy Optimization (PARPO),
28:which decouples generic task-quality rewards from personalized preference re-
29:wards and uses user-specific anchors to stabilize learning under heterogeneous
30:reward scales. We further introduce a two-stage preference-disentangled reward
31:model and Preference-Aligned Skill Evolution Graph Memory (PSGM) for per-
32:sonalized supervision and preference-aligned skill retrieval. Together, they form a
33:closed loop of preference identification, policy optimization, and structured skill
34:accumulation. Experiments on ETAPP, ETAPP-Hard, and SJAgent show that our
35:framework consistently outperforms strong memory and RL baselines. Code and
36:data are included in the supplementary materials.

38:Introduction
39:Large language model (LLM)-based agentic reinforcement learning (Agentic RL) has emerged as
40:a powerful paradigm for optimizing LLM agents, achieving strong performance in code genera-
41:tion [Gehring et al., 2025, Le et al., 2022, Yang et al., 2024], web navigation [Nakano et al., 2021,
42:Qi et al., 2025], tool use [Qian et al., 2026, Feng et al., 2026a, Xia et al., 2026], and long-horizon
43:planning [Tang et al., 2026, Peng et al., 2026, Xi et al., 2026]. However, most of this progress
44:has been made in verifiable settings [Qian et al., 2026, Jin et al., 2025, Shao et al., 2024], where
45:policy optimization can rely on a unique ground-truth answer. In contrast, in many real-world agent
46:applications—such as e-commerce assistance, travel planning, and daily scheduling [Skarlinski
47:et al., 2024, Schmidgall et al., 2024, Ning et al., 2025, Xie et al., 2024, Lyu et al., 2026]—this
48:verifiability breaks down because optimal behavior is user-dependent: the same query may admit
49:multiple plausible trajectories, with the preferred one determined by the user’s preferences, habits,
50:and constraints.
51:Recent work has begun to extend LLM optimization beyond strictly verifiable tasks. Non-verifiable

89:PARPO preserves general task competence while enabling user-contingent policy improvement. To
90:provide cleaner personalized supervision, we develop a two-stage preference-disentangled reward
91:model that separates intrinsic interests from conformity and contextual effects. To support personal-
92:ized rollout context, we introduce Preference-Aligned Skill Evolution Graph Memory (PSGM), an
93:evolving heterogeneous graph memory that organizes users, skills, tools, scenarios, and trajectories
94:for preference-aligned skill retrieval. Together, these components form a closed loop of preference
95:identification, personalized policy optimization, and structured skill accumulation. Our contributions
96:are summarized as follows:
97:• We formulate personalized Agentic RL for user-conditioned agent tasks, where optimal behavior
98:depends on individual preferences.
99:• We propose PARPO, an anchor-stabilized and reward-decoupled policy optimization method for
100:learning personalized policies under heterogeneous user reward scales.
101:• We introduce a preference-disentangled reward model and PSGM to provide reliable personalized
102:supervision and preference-aligned skill retrieval.
103:• We evaluate our framework on ETAPP [Hao et al., 2025], ETAPP-Hard, and SJAgent (a real-world
104:industrial agent training scenario from a large Chinese e-commerce platform), showing gains in
105:personalization and procedural quality while maintaining factual and logical quality.

--METHOD--
316:Method
317:To address the challenge of personalized decision-making in user-conditioned tasks, we propose a
318:unified Agentic RL framework. As illustrated in Figure 2, the framework operates as a closed loop of
319:personalized retrieval, generation, evaluation, and refinement. Given a user query and profile, the
320:framework first retrieves relevant historical skills from a graph-based memory to form a personalized
321:rollout context. Conditioned on this enriched context, the LLM-based policy interacts with the
322:environment to generate decision-making trajectories. These trajectories are then evaluated by a
323:dual-reward system: a general task-quality reward and a personalized preference reward. Finally, the
324:policy is updated using these decoupled signals, and high-value trajectories are consolidated back
325:into memory as reusable skills.
326:In the following, we first introduce the core of our framework—the policy optimization algorithm
327:(PARPO, §4.1). We then detail the two supporting modules that make this optimization possible: the
328:personalized reward model that provides user-conditioned signals (§4.2), and the skill graph memory
329:that structures the rollout context (§4.3).
330:4.1
331:PARPO: Personalized Anchor Reward-Decoupled Policy Optimization
332:We now introduce the policy optimizer in our framework. PARPO separates task quality from
333:personalized alignment, allowing the policy to improve user-specific behavior without entangling
334:it with shared supervision. In implementation, PARPO is instantiated as a dual-track GRPO-style
335:advantage estimator built on the personalized reward model in Section 4.2 and the graph memory in
336:Section 4.3.
337:Theoretical justification.
338:Appendix C shows that under heterogeneous user preferences, personal-
339:ized optimization is preferable to user-agnostic optimization, while standard GRPO incurs structural
340:bias from pooled baselines and normalization. The analysis yields three conclusions. First, under
341:heterogeneous user preferences, user-aware optimization is never worse than user-agnostic optimiza-
342:tion: when different users prefer different trajectories for the same query, a single average-optimal
343:policy necessarily compromises across users. Second, standard GRPO introduces structural bias in
344:personalized settings because it uses pooled baselines and pooled normalization statistics. As a result,
345:its advantage estimate can deviate from the true user-specific advantage due to both reward-center
346:mismatch and reward-scale mismatch, with the dominant error controlled by cross-user preference
347:heterogeneity. Third, PARPO reduces this bias by decoupling generic task-quality rewards from
348:personalized preference rewards and calibrating the personalized branch with user-specific anchors.

647:Baselines
648:We compare against prompting, memory, RL, and memory-RL baselines, including REACT, MEM0,

652:variant in the ablation study, which removes PARPO’s user-anchor calibration while keeping the
653:same personalized reward and memory components. For baselines in personalized environments, we
654:additionally provide the native personalization reward to ensure fair comparison.
655:5.1.2
656:Benchmarks, Evaluation, and Training Details
657:We evaluate on ETAPP and SJAgent. ETAPP is a public benchmark for personal assistant agents,
658:covering user behaviors in daily-life scenarios; we further construct a more challenging split, ETAPP-
659:Hard. SJAgent is a realistic environment for merchant decision-making and recommendation, built
660:from merchant data on a major Chinese e-commerce platform. Environment details are provided in
661:Appendix D.2, and the construction pipeline of ETAPP-Hard is given in Appendix D.3.
662:For ETAPP, we report Judge, Personal, Proactive, and Procedure. For SJAgent, we report Reward,
663:Data Authenticity, Business Logic, Merchant Profile Match, Task Completion, and Market Anal-
664:ysis Depth. Metric definitions, official evaluation prompts, and scoring criteria are provided in
665:Appendix D.4, and training hyperparameters are listed in Appendix D.5.

1575:Conclusion and Limitations
1576:This work studied personalized Agentic RL for user-conditioned tasks, where optimal behavior
1577:depends on user preferences rather than a single correct answer. We proposed a unified framework
1578:centered on PARPO, which decouples general-quality and personalized rewards and stabilizes training
1579:with user-specific anchors. Experiments on ETAPP, ETAPP-Hard, and SJAgent showed consistent
1580:and significant gains in personalization while preserving overall task quality, highlighting the value
1581:of training-time personalized optimization for user-centric agents. A key limitation of this work is
1582:the scale of the human evaluation: due to annotation cost and limited expert availability, we reported
1583:judgments from only 15 experts on 20 randomly sampled examples. Although these results broadly
1584:agree with the LLM evaluations, future work should expand the human study to a larger and more
1585:diverse pool of annotators and evaluation instances to better assess robustness, generalizability, and
1586:real-world validity.
