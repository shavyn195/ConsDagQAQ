32:ABSTRACT
33:Long-horizon language-model agents trained with reinforcement learning often
34:receive sparse outcome rewards that do not reveal which decisions along a tra-
35:jectory deserve credit. Episode-level advantages provide coarse trajectory-wide
36:credit, while step-level comparisons offer finer resolution with context-dependent
37:estimation noise. We propose Granularity-Adaptive Credit Assignment (GACA),
38:a critic-free method that adaptively mixes episode- and step-level credit for each
39:decision during policy optimization. GACA normalizes the sampled response’s
40:mean per-token negative log-likelihood (NLL) within each trajectory and uses the
41:resulting criticality score to determine the step-specific mixture. The computation
42:reuses rollout log-probabilities without additional training rollouts or model eval-
43:uations. Our analysis characterizes optimal score-dependent mixing and derives
44:conditions linking expected NLL to a lower bound on the preferred step-level
45:weight. Across ALFWorld and WebShop with 1.5B and 7B backbones, GACA
46:achieves the highest reported mean success rates among the compared methods,
47:while introducing negligible additional computation.

49:INTRODUCTION
50:Language-model agents act over many steps to complete household tasks, browse web stores, and
51:use external tools (Yao et al., 2022b; Shridhar et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2022a). Reinforcement learning
52:can improve these agents using task outcomes (Ouyang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025), but a sparse
53:terminal reward leaves a difficult credit-assignment problem: which decisions should receive the
54:learning signal? A trajectory can contain both consequential choices and actions whose alternatives
55:would have little effect on the outcome (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019).
56:Critic-free methods avoid training a value model by comparing several rollouts of the same task.
57:GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) computes an episode-level relative advantage and broadcasts it to every
58:action. GiGPO (Feng et al., 2026) adds local resolution by grouping steps with the same anchor
59:observation and comparing their continuation returns. Its fixed combination of episode- and step-
60:level signals leaves a further question: should every decision rely on these two credit estimates in
61:the same proportion?
62:The two granularities offer a decision-dependent estimation trade-off. Episode credit spreads the
63:outcome over all decisions, while step credit separates local continuations using finite, context-
64:dependent comparison groups.

17:Abstract
18:Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) trains a large language model (LLM) to act
19:over long, multi-step interactions. However, a single localized error can cause
20:task failure, while trajectory-level rewards provide limited guidance for assigning
21:credit to individual decisions. To address this limitation, we introduce Segment-
22:level Hindsight Advantage Reweighting for Policy Optimization (SHARPO), a
23:credit-assignment mechanism that refines Group Relative Policy Optimization
24:(GRPO) at the level of environment-facing segments. Inspired by the existing
25:on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) method, SHARPO computes teacher–student
26:log-probability gaps within each segment and uses the resulting signal to compute
27:a bounded multiplier on the GRPO advantage. This multiplier is shared by all
28:tokens within the segment, allowing credit to vary across different segments. With
29:Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, SHARPO outperforms existing baselines on the ALFWorld
30:and WebShop benchmarks, including GRPO, SDAR, RLSD, and StepOPSD.

32:Introduction
33:Post-training plays a key role in adapting large language models (LLMs) to specific tasks and
34:improving their performance in interactive environments [Ouyang et al., 2022, Jin et al., 2025].
35:Reinforcement learning (RL) supports this adaptation by optimizing agent behavior using feedback
36:from task outcomes. When these outcomes can be automatically verified, their correctness provides
37:a direct reward signal for training, as demonstrated in mathematical reasoning and search-based
38:question answering [Guo et al., 2025a, Jin et al., 2025]. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
39:offers an efficient approach to learning from such rewards without training a separate value network
40:[Shao et al., 2024]. For each task, GRPO samples a group of rollouts and normalizes their rewards
41:within the group to estimate advantages. Under outcome supervision, each rollout receives a single
42:advantage that is shared by all of its generated tokens.
43:In multi-turn interactions, however, an agent must make a sequence of decisions before receiving
44:feedback on the final outcome. In this case, the rewards are sparse and are available only at the end
45:of an episode, hence they provide little direct information about the contribution of each intermediate
46:step to that outcome. For example, a shopping agent may find a suitable product but select an incorrect
47:option before completing the purchase. If the resulting trajectory receives a negative advantage,
48:both the useful search and the mistaken selection inherit the same negative coefficient. The shared
49:advantage therefore does not distinguish actions that helped accomplish the task from those that led
50:to failure. This motivates us to design a credit-assignment mechanism in RL that uses finer-grained

14:Abstract
15:An LLM multi-agent system (MAS) is only as good as the agents it chooses to trust:
16:its accuracy and cost hinge on its topology, meaning which agents are included
17:and how they communicate. A single task score, however, does not reveal which
18:agents help or hinder performance. We propose Contrastive Attribution Topology
19:Search (CATS), which searches for compact teams within a fixed supergraph of
20:specialized agents. An LLM attributer compares execution traces across candidate
21:topologies and assigns credit to individual agents. CATS uses these credits to update
22:supergraph edges while progressively pruning weak edges and agents, yielding a
23:small team for deployment. With small open-weight models and matched candidate
24:evaluations, CATS improves mean accuracy on BigCodeBench by 5.3 percentage
25:points over score-only heuristic search while using 54% fewer deployment tokens.
26:On MBPP, CATS matches the strongest baselines with fewer tokens than the
27:other searches. In a HotpotQA prompt-poisoning stress test, CATS excludes all
28:adversarially prompted agents in all three runs, whereas random search retains some
29:at comparable accuracy. Per-agent credit thus supports both team optimization and
30:the pruning of potentially harmful agents.
31:1
32:Introduction
33:LLM-based multi-agent systems split work across specialized agents, for example one that writes
34:code and another that tests it. Such collaboration can improve small models [9, 10], but more agents
35:and messages mean more tokens. Building a good system therefore requires choosing which agents
36:to include and how they communicate, that is, the topology. Since the best topology depends on the
37:task, manual design is difficult [5, 6], which motivates automated search over candidate teams.
38:Task scores, however, evaluate the team as a whole: a correct solution may contain unnecessary work,
39:and a failed one may include useful contributions. Scores alone thus say little about which agents to
40:keep. Can feedback on individual agents guide the search for compact, effective teams?
41:We introduce Contrastive Attribution Topology Search (CATS), an offline meta-agent that assembles
42:teams from a fixed agent pool. Starting from a supergraph of candidate links, CATS evaluates
43:sampled teams on target-domain tasks. An LLM compares their transcripts and assigns each agent
44:signed credit. CATS then updates link probabilities, prunes weak links and unreachable agents, and

284:Method
285:CATS repeatedly samples teams from a supergraph, credits each agent, and prunes weak edges.
286:Supergraph. We search for an optimized multi-agent topology as a subgraph of a hand-crafted
287:complete directed acyclic graph specialized to a chosen problem domain (Figure 1). Its nodes are
288:agents and its edges denote direct communication (and thereby the scheduling order). Agents are
289:specialized via system prompts, optional reasoning, and tool sets. We hand-craft them using domain
290:knowledge (automating this is left to future work). Each edge e carries a logit θe with sampling
291:probability αe = σ(θe), initialized to a common α0. Following DARTS [7], we relax the discrete
292:edge choices to these continuous parameters but replace gradient descent with heuristic credit-based
293:updates derived from contrastive attribution.
294:Sampling. At each step, we sample K topologies by independently drawing e ∼Bernoulli(αe),
295:rejecting graphs with nodes unreachable from the source. Since agents share an environment, not
296:every agent needs a downstream consumer: edges are optional communication channels. We evaluate
297:each candidate on N tasks, obtaining scores sk,q. Candidates, tasks, and often agents run in parallel.
298:Attribution. An LLM attributer assigns each participating agent i in candidate k on task q a credit
299:ck,q,i ∈[−1, 1] (harmful to helpful) by comparing candidate rollouts on the same task. The attributer
300:sees task scores, length-capped agent outputs, tool calls, and the final artifact state (e.g., the coding
301:environment), and runs in parallel across tasks.

483:Limitations, Responsible Use, and Conclusion
484:Evaluating a single model family across three domains and three seeds limits what we can conclude
485:about broader transfer and variance, while high baseline scores on MBPP leave little headroom for
486:accuracy gains. Moreover, although pruning yields sparse coding teams, this may reflect coordination
487:via the shared environment rather than a general lack of need for direct communication. Finally, we
488:only track token cost without optimizing it, making Pareto search over score and cost, as done for
489:prompts by Büssing et al. [2], a natural next step.
490:Attribution credits have not been validated against human judgments. Credit–success correlation
491:(Table 9) is only a consistency check, since the attributer sees system_score. The score-only
492:baseline was not separately tuned and does not isolate which component of CATS accounts for the
493:difference. Credit is node-level, propagated equally to all incoming fired edges. A comparison with
494:Graph-GRPO’s statistical edge-level credit remains future work [3].
495:CATS turns a hand-crafted supergraph into a small fixed team. It improved the balance between
496:performance and efficiency on BigCodeBench, reduced unnecessary complexity on the easier MBPP,
497:and excluded poisoned agents that task scores alone could not detect. In all three domains it matched
498:or beat score-only heuristic search on score, deployment-token use, or both. This supports attribution
499:for team optimization and the pruning of potentially harmful agents, not a security guarantee.
500:Responsible-use statement.
501:Selecting a topology determines which agents gain access to tools and
502:task context. Our stress test shows that attribution can exclude fixed adversarial prompts, but this
503:does not establish deployment safety. An adaptive adversary could act beyond the capped transcript
504:window or exploit the unverified LLM attributer directly. Tool and data permissions must therefore
505:remain outside the optimizer.

28:INTRODUCTION
29:Large Language Models (LLMs) ([3, 5, 6, 26, 36]) have been at the forefront of advancing artificial
30:intelligence, marking significant breakthroughs in diverse fields. The planning capabilities of LLMs,
31:particularly their ability to use tools ([33, 34]), enable them not only to execute commands and
32:perform web searches but also to enhance their advanced mathematical reasoning abilities. LLM
33:Compiler [12] and its subsequent work ([7, 8]) propose constructing tooling usage as a directed acyclic
34:graph (DAG) to enable the parallel execution of independent tools, thereby improving tool-calling
35:efficiency. CodeAct [28] and CodePlan [29] propose leveraging the generation of pseudo-Python code
36:to outline high-level reasoning processes for complex multi-step reasoning tasks, where each tool
37:usage is represented as a function call within the code. ReWOO [30] proposes a modular framework
38:that decouples the reasoning process from the external observations of each tool usage, thereby
39:reducing token consumption and improving efficiency. [15] clusters the provided tools into groups
40:of toolkits, plans at the toolkit level, and replans by selecting tools within the same toolkit if error
41:comes out. [17] proposes a method called Predictive-Decoding, which leverages Model Predictive
42:Control from the optimal control field to mitigate early errors in planning and promote non-myopic
43:planning, thereby enhancing overall accuracy. ReasonFlux [31] proposes a framework in which the
44:LLM reasons over template fields, executes tools based on the templates, and employs reinforcement
45:learning to improve planning accuracy using an action completion reward.

47:capable of planning, tool use, and multi-step (also multi-turn) execution. The introduction of Group
48:Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [22] further inspired the development of Reinforcement

121:Tool Execution as DAG
122:Given a plan p generated by the LLM, we represent it as a directed graph G = (V, E), where
123:V = (v1, . . . , vn) is the set of nodes and E = (e1, . . . , em) is the set of edges. The node v1
124:corresponds to the user query, and vn represents the final node that aggregates observations and
125:returns the response. The intermediate nodes v2, . . . , vn−1 correspond to tool calls, each associated
126:with an attribute that stores its tool payload in JSON format. An edge ei ∈E denotes a dependency
127:between two tools, where an output key from the source tool serves as an input key to the target tool.
128:We represent the tool graph as an ordered list of tasks in a JSON-like text style. Each task contains
129:four fields : task_id, toolname, payload, and dependencies. A task can be associated with
130:multiple dependencies. A task can be expressed as {task_id: task_4, toolname: name, payload:
131:{param1: val1, param2: $2.outputkey1, param3: $3.outputkey4}, dependencies: [task_2,
132:task_3]}.

134:METHODOLOGY
135:In real-world domains, API specifications and orchestrations are often considerably more complex,
136:making it challenging for LLMs pretrained on general public data to generate accurate and reliable
137:plans for diverse user queries. Drawing inspiration from LLMCompiler [12, 8], for queries involving
138:complex tool interactions, it is advantageous to first construct a tool execution DAG. This DAG serves
139:as a blueprint for executing tools sequentially or in parallel, with subsequent replanning guided by
140:both the execution results of the current DAG and any new user queries in later turns.
141:3.1
142:OrchDAG – Synthetic Data Generation Pipeline for Multi-Turn Tool Use
143:As discussed in Section 1, the design of the data generation pipeline follows several key principles to
144:better reflect real-world tool-use scenarios: (1) the complexity of the tool execution DAG for each
145:synthetic user query should be controllable through pipeline hyperparameters, (2) the system prompt
146:provided to the LLM must include irrelevant tools (both schema and description) so that the model
147:learns to identify and select only the relevant ones, (3) the output payload of each tool should contain
148:multiple fields, typically four or five, and (4) at least one key output field from a tool should serve as
149:an input to another tool, but with a different field name, to capture schema misalignment commonly
150:observed in practice.
151:Moreover, in multi-turn settings, the data should capture scenarios where certain nodes in the tool
152:execution DAG fail. When the user issues a follow-up query, the corresponding DAG in the dataset
153:should exclude nodes that have already been executed in the previous turn, while reusing their
154:available results whenever applicable. In light of these requirements, we develop a graph-based data
155:generation pipeline implemented with LangGraph 2, accompanied by a set of validation functions to
156:ensure the quality of the generated data points.
157:As shown in Figure 1, the data generation pipeline begins by collecting real high-quality tools
158:from existing benchmarks, Specifically, we leverage APIGEN [16] and TOOLACE [14]. To ensure
159:sufficient complexity, we retain only examples where the final answer involves more than two distinct
160:functions represented in JSON format. Notably, we extract tools directly from the answers rather than
161:from the system-prompt tool lists. This design choice eliminates the need for additional categorization
162:or clustering steps, which could introduce unnecessary uncertainty, while naturally yielding a smaller
163:and more coherent set of related tools. After filtering, APIGEN contributes 2,542 data points and
164:TOOLACE contributes 1,005.
165:For each data point, suppose it contains four real tools; these are placed as the first layer of the tool
166:execution DAG. Based on the hyperparameters of the DAG (height and width), we then randomly
167:sample a topological order to obtain a DAG template (see Figure 1). According to this template,
168:we synthesize the tools for each node layer by layer under the following conditions: (1) each input
169:key must depend on an output key from one of its parent nodes in the DAG, and (2) the field names
170:should not remain identical across instances but instead vary randomly. After populating the DAG
171:template, we obtain the tool execution DAG, which is then used to prompt the LLM to generate
172:the corresponding user query, conditioned on the DAG and a few-shot set of examples. Finally, we

12:Self-improving AI systems aim to reduce reliance on human engineering by learning to improve
13:their own learning and problem-solving processes. Existing approaches to recursive self-improvement
14:typically rely on fixed, handcrafted meta-level mechanisms, which fundamentally limit how fast such
15:systems can improve. The Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) (Zhang et al., 2025b) demonstrates that
16:open-ended self-improvement is achievable in coding. Starting from a single coding agent, the DGM
17:repeatedly generates and evaluates self-modified variants, forming a growing archive of stepping stones
18:for future improvement. Because both evaluation and self-modification are coding tasks, gains in
19:coding ability can translate into gains in self-improvement ability. However, this alignment does
20:not generally hold beyond coding domains. We introduce hyperagents, self-referential agents that
21:integrate a task agent (which solves the target task) and a meta agent (which modifies itself and the
22:task agent) into a single editable program. Crucially, the meta-level modification procedure is itself
23:editable, enabling metacognitive self-modification, improving not only task-solving behavior, but also
24:the mechanism that generates future improvements. We instantiate this framework by extending DGM
25:to create DGM-Hyperagents (DGM-H). By allowing the improvement procedure to evolve, the DGM-H
26:eliminates the assumption of domain-specific alignment between task performance and self-modification
27:skill, and can potentially support self-accelerating progress on any computable task. Across diverse
28:domains (coding, paper review, robotics reward design, and Olympiad-level math-solution grading),
29:the DGM-H improves performance over time and outperforms baselines without self-improvement or
30:open-ended exploration, as well as prior self-improving systems like DGM. We further show that the
31:DGM-H improves the process by which it generates new agents (e.g., persistent memory, performance
32:tracking), and that these meta-level improvements transfer across domains and accumulate across
33:runs. All experiments were conducted with safety precautions (e.g., sandboxing, human oversight).
34:We discuss what safety entails in this setting and the broader implications of self-improving systems.

350:Baselines
351:We compare the Darwin Gödel Machine with Hyperagents (DGM-H) against a set of baselines designed
352:to isolate the contributions of self-improving meta agents, open-ended exploration, and metacognitive self-
353:modification (i.e., hyperagents) (Appendix B). DGM-H without self-improving meta agents (DGM-H w/o
354:self-improve) fixes the meta agent modifying other hyperagents to the initial meta agent for the entire
355:run. While the system can still generate and evaluate new hyperagents, the procedures used to propose
356:and apply modifications are not themselves modifiable. This baseline replicates the approach of (ADAS,
357:Hu et al., 2025) in this setting. It tests whether performance gains arise solely from task-level optimization
358:under a fixed meta-level mechanism, or from improvements in the self-improvement process itself. DGM-H
359:without open-ended exploration (DGM-H w/o open-ended exploration) removes the archive of previously
360:generated hyperagents. Instead of retaining previous variants as stepping stones for future exploration, each
361:newly generated hyperagent replaces its predecessor. After generating a new hyperagent, it is evaluated and
362:automatically becomes the next selected parent. This baseline isolates the role of open-ended, population-based
363:search. Comparing against this baseline tests whether sustained improvement requires accumulating and
364:reusing diverse intermediate solutions.
365:We also compare against the original implementation of the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) (Zhang et al.,
366:2025b). The DGM relies on a handcrafted, fixed mechanism to generate self-improvement instructions.
367:Because this instruction-generation mechanism is designed specifically for coding benchmarks and is not
368:modifiable, the DGM’s self-improvement capacity in other domains is limited (Section 2). To provide a stronger
369:comparison, we manually customize the DGM’s instruction-generation mechanism for the target domains
370:(DGM-custom) (Appendix B). This baseline measures how much the DGM relies on human engineering to
371:remain competitive across domains. Comparing the DGM-H against this baseline tests whether automated
372:metacognitive self-modification can outperform human-designed self-improvement mechanisms. Additionally,

--LIMITATIONS--
836:Limitations and Conclusion
837:This work introduces hyperagents and incorporates them into the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) to form DGM-
838:Hyperagents (DGM-H). DGM-H is a general self-improvement framework that open-endedly evolves an archive
839:of self-improving hyperagents for any computable task, enabling the system to improve both task performance
840:and its own self-improvement mechanism. Across diverse domains, the DGM-H produced substantial and
841:generalizable gains in task performance while also improving its ability to generate improvements, with these
842:meta-level gains transferring across domains and compounding across runs.
843:Our results suggest that self-improvements can compound across different experimental settings, but this
844:version of DGM-H has limitations that constrain truly unbounded progress. First, it operates with a fixed
845:task distribution. One direction is to co-evolve the task distribution by generating new tasks and curricula
846:that adapt to the agent’s capabilities (Clune, 2019; Zhang et al., 2024; Faldor et al., 2025; Bolton et al., 2025).
847:Second, components of the open-ended exploration loop (e.g., parent selection, evaluation protocols) remain
848:fixed. Although hyperagents can modify their self-improvement mechanisms, they cannot alter the outer
849:process that determines which agents are selected or how they are evaluated. Keeping these components fixed
850:improves experimental stability and safety, but limits full self-modifiability. Enabling hyperagents to modify
851:these outer-loop components and adapt their own search strategy and evaluation process is another promising
852:direction for future work. Our preliminary results suggest such extensions are feasible (Appendix E.5). We
853:discuss further limitations in Appendix F.
854:DGM-H demonstrates that open-ended self-improvement can be made practical across diverse domains.
855:Provided sufficient safety considerations are worked out, the DGM-H suggests a path toward safe self-
856:accelerating systems that not only search for better solutions, but continually improve their ability to
857:self-improve.
